# Four Hundred and Ten

The water was at the third step and had been at the third step for nine days. Ilse counted the steps every morning on the way in, the way other people weighed themselves.

She left her boots in the silt in the lobby. Up the ladder, the second floor of the Marram exchange was dry and smelled of hot dust and warm copper, and three gulls sat in the dark of the stairwell waiting her out. Under the window, with a clear line to the door: tablet, headphones, a mug with a chip out of the rim, the crate of drives under her elbow. Six weeks of salvage behind her, eleven ahead. On the door, printed in 1998 in the flat vowels of a dead century: IN CASE OF FLOOD, DO NOT USE ELEVATOR. There was one elevator in Marram, and it had been in the harbor since February.

The county called the work unsent correspondence and paid her by the drive. Marram's trunk line had kept running nineteen months after the fiber went — an old buffer that held the calls the exchange could not route and released them, slowly, into a town with no subscribers left on it. Ilse took them out one at a time and gave them numbers. That was the work. It was not a metaphor, though nobody at the Resilience Office had quite believed that. Six weeks in they sent a man to ask whether the archive might be put in the cloud. It had been raining for eleven days. The roof of the exchange was by then a rumor.

She wiped the tablet on her trousers and opened the day's seventh drive. A woman: "Tell him the fence is down, and he's going to be eighty." Ilse logged it, and the eight seconds it took, and the fact that it had been sent four times. Then a boy saying nothing at all for four minutes, breathing in a room with a window open and, far off, a football match.

Module seven came out of the east wall with a sound like a spoon in a jar. Salt had grown a white fur over the pins, which in the light turned out to be small exact trees, each one a crystal with branches. She wiped the screen where her thumb would be. Where she had touched it, a bloom came up on the glass and stayed. She scraped it off with a thumbnail and it came away dry, like scurf, and while she watched, it began to come back.

At 9:14 the buffer stamped something coming up the trunk.

It was not on a drive. It was coming through the line itself. Ilse put one earphone in, held the other against the jack, where the salt had crusted, and heard her own voice.

*Ilse. It's the ninth. The water's at the third step, I know. There are four hundred and ten of them in the east corridor, and they're not records. They're just people who got cut off. I've moved them upstairs. The seal's going in at eleven tonight, so if you're hearing this on this floor, you're weeks early. Go—*

She played it eleven times. The stamp put the call nine years, four months and change ahead of her. She wiped at the screen with her sleeve, and the salt came up again under it.

What she had was this: a dry floor, a finished index, her own voice on the other side of the seal, still in it, still doing it, not sorry. *Weeks early.* There was more time than she had thought. For six weeks she had shut the county's book at six and gone back up the ladder at seven. That evening she put her boots on and did not go back up the ladder.

Her sister called at ten past nine, when Ilse was already in the room above the pharmacy.

"Four hundred and six," Wren said. "You said four hundred and six."

"I said four hundred and ten."

"You said—" Wren stopped. Wren had been packing the flat for a month. "You've finished them," she said, in the voice you use for a hospital.

"I said four hundred and ten," Ilse said, and went to bed and slept all the way through, which she had not done since March.

For three days she worked to six and stopped at six. On the third evening she carried the recorder into the east corridor and set it by the trunk. The crates stood along the wall where they always had. She sat down, opened the log on her knees, and counted the names by the blue light of the tablet. She had never counted them; the log counted messages. Four hundred and ten people, cut off, every one of them in her index.

Then she took a pin to the jack and worked the crust out of it, and the crackle went out of the line as if a window had been shut somewhere.

*—home. Let the water have the rest of it. I've done the part that counted.*

Rain was easing on the roof above them. A long pause. Then, closer to the phone:

*It's stopped raining. Go home, Ilse.*

She had heard *upstairs* as a dry floor: a place ten years on where the work would be finished and worth doing. There was no upstairs. There was a woman on the last night, wet to the elbow, giving the rest of it away, and the rest of it was only the counting.

Ilse put the pin back in her pocket and picked up the first crate.

They went out to the hill in seven trips on the pharmacy's handcart. She put them in the old lifeboat house, above the water line of anything the sea had got round to yet, and the door still had a lock on it. The last one she set down gently. She peeled the last label off the county's roll and stuck it to the wood and wrote nothing on it.

Her recorder she left on top, and by the time she had her coat buttoned, salt had come up out of its port — white and branching, exact, in a shape like the street plan of Marram.

The gulls were back on the east breakwater. They had not been there in months. She watched them work the tide until she was cold. Then she went down the far side of the hill to Wren's, where the kitchen light was on.

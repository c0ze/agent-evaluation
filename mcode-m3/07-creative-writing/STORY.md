# What Salt Keeps

The recorder was older than it should have been. Mara found it inside a cutlery drawer in a house on Fennel Street, beneath a tea strainer and a folded letter no one had ever sent. The brand was a Czech one she half-remembered from childhood, and the rubber around the play key had gone brittle to the touch, like a dried apricot. She brought it back to the truck, set it on a folded beach towel to keep it from rolling, and plugged in a borrowed cable. The small screen bloomed orange. Eleven messages. The first from March.

She listened to them in order.

A mother reminding her son to pick her up. A man apologizing for a thing he could not name. A woman leaving a number twice because she had forgotten she had done it the first time. A child counting to ten very slowly and then not finishing. Each one was a person the town had agreed, by the slow ritual of leaving, to lose the right to call back. Mara's job — what was left of a job — was to make sure they existed somewhere. She pressed record on her field unit and let them play through.

The salt came in on the sea breeze and sat, fine as confectioners' sugar, on every flat surface. By Wednesday the paperbacks in the library had gone soft at the corners. By Thursday the new capacitors on the truck's dashboard had thrown a hissy fit. Mara kept a small artist's brush in her shirt pocket and ran it along the seams of the recorder before every listen. The crystals came off in pale dunes, as if the device were slowly being buried.

She made it to message eleven before she heard her own voice.

"— Mara?"

The room was hers. The truck cab. The hum of the inverter. She knew the way she asked her own name when she came in tired from grocery runs, half-hoping nobody would answer. She knew the small polite breath before she spoke. The timestamp said ten years from next week.

She did not transcribe it right away. She set the recorder down on the towel. She went outside and stood on the seawall with the brush in her hand. Below her, the road that had been a road two winters ago was now a kind of estuary, the tide taking liberties with the asphalt. A heron stood on a stop sign as if considering the turn lane. The heron, she decided, had the better view.

Back inside, she put on headphones.

"— Mara? It is — the line is bad. I don't have long. Listen. Don't transcribe the second-to-last message. The man. You'll know him. Don't keep it. Let it be the only thing that doesn't—"

A pause. A breath that could have been a laugh if it had been less tired.

The recording cut off there. Not in a glitch but in the particular way a person put down a phone when they had decided to stop talking. Eleven messages. She had not been sure the town was worth eleven messages.

She thought, for an hour, that her future self was warning her. That she was being told, in a voice she had not yet earned, to let a man keep his unsaid thing in a drawer somewhere forever, a small conspiracy against her own life's work. She sat with it the way she sat with the recordings themselves: not listening for the meaning, only for the grain of the voice beneath it. The brush in her pocket. The salt on the window.

Then she played it a third time.

It was the cough. A small, almost nothing of a cough, two words in, that the archivist ten years older had not bothered to suppress. The cough of someone sitting in a place where the air was not the air of here. She had not thought to ask, until that third play, what room the woman on the recording had been sitting in. The truck's inverter? No. The inverter would have hummed. The cab would have creaked in the wind. None of that was there.

She was not in this town.

She was not, perhaps, anywhere she had a window.

Mara sat very still. The recorder's screen dimmed. Outside, a gull complained about nothing in particular, and somewhere a door — a real one, with hinges — let go of a long sigh. She understood, then, that the message had not been a warning at all. It had been a small kindness from a woman who had outlived the room she had been standing in, who had carried one unsaid thing with her for ten years and was asking, in the only way the device allowed, that she herself be allowed to carry the other one forward.

She picked up the field unit. She pressed record. The man who could not name the thing he was sorry for was still on the recorder. Mara did not transcribe him. She left him in the device, exactly where he had been left for her, a small parcel of salt and silence in the long pocket of the town's leaving. When she turned the truck north at the end of the week, the recorder rode beside her on the seat, the brush laid across it as if for a small ceremony, and the salt on the windows of the cab, where she could see it, came off in pale dunes whenever the light was kind.

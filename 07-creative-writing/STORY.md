# What the Salt Keeps

The salt had grown across the circuit board in the shape of a small white tree. Nell tilted the drive under the lamp and the tree became a fern, then a coastline. She worked the toothbrush along the contacts and the little forests broke onto the newspaper, where they made a new, smaller shore.

The generator outside coughed, caught, held. The room smelled of wet rope and hot dust.

On the desk lay the morning's intake: three cassettes in a shoebox, a battery recorder in a plastic bag. For eleven months the town had been sending her the things it had never sent anyone — voicemails saved instead of delivered, tapes left in drawers. Mrs. Almeida had brought in her dead husband's: forty years of "the boiler's making a noise."

Nell had built the digitizer from a salvaged tower and two drives in a biscuit tin lined with newspaper. Every morning she cleaned the salt off them; every morning it grew back.

"You look like you're gardening," said Barrow from the doorway. He was the council's retreat officer; he measured how fast the town was leaving. "How's the archive?"

"Growing."

"Everything's growing." He set his clipboard on the desk, took it back, and left, with the air of a man who had decided the paperwork could say it.

In March the road to the point had been closed twice. The council painted a green line to mark the new shoreline; rain ran it into the ditch; Barrow repainted it; the sea came up under it anyway. People took their doors when they left. Nell knew because they told her into the recorder, as if the doors were relatives they were obliged to mention.

The message with the wrong date was in the last cassette. Only the case carried a strip of masking tape: 11.04.36, in Nell's handwriting — the seven a little cross, the four open at the top — hers, and no one else's. Ten years from now. But the ink had browned at the edges; the date had been written a long time ago.

She wound the tape back. The voice was hers.

"It's me," it said. "Don't be angry. I know I left it late. The wall went on a Tuesday — the studio's half gone, and the salt's got into everything. Even the coffee machine. I never fixed the little screen. It just says E. I got most of the drives out. I left the rest. There wasn't time."

Under the voice, wind got in somewhere; something loose tapped. Her own cold was in it, the catch she'd had since November.

"You'll have found the one I left on the kitchen table by now. The soup one. You always did find what I hid. I'm not going to say I did this for the town. I did it for you."

A pause, then quieter: "There's nothing left here to keep. I'm coming home. Don't wait up."

The reel clicked and ran out.

Nell sat with her hand on the stop button. What killed her was the tone. She had never heard herself sound so finished. The drives left behind, the flat "I left the rest." Ten years from now she would be a woman in a half-drowned room telling herself it was all right to stop, asking forgiveness for a life of failed preservations.

She thought of the green line, the three hundred voices in the index saying I meant to tell you. What did it matter, if she let them go?

She did not digitize the rest of the box that day. She put the cassette in the biscuit tin, on top of the drives, and could not have said why — only that no one else should hear it.

At home the kettle clicked off. She ate at the kitchen table, where the recorder had been lying for six weeks. She had spoken one message into it: "I made soup, the kind you liked, with the little noodles." Then she had stopped: what came next was either Come home or I'm fine, don't worry, and she could not bear the space between them.

Jo had left in the spring, of her own accord — the only way Nell would have let her. No fight, only a slow accumulation of things unsaid, a little each day, until the circuit was gone.

On the third morning the coffee machine's little screen began to show E, just as the voice had said, and Nell stood with the toothbrush in her hand and laughed once, without humor, at how thorough the salt was. She played the cassette again.

"You always did find what I hid," the tape said.

She had heard it as said to herself — she was always losing things, always finding them in the last place. But in the corner of the cassette case was a small J, in her handwriting, that she had looked at and not seen. Only one person had ever found what Nell hid.

Jo at nine, finding the birthday present three days early. Jo at seventeen, finding letters Nell had forgotten she'd written. Jo who had never heard the messages, because Nell had never sent the only one that mattered.

The voice was asking, ten years from now, had she found it? Had Jo found the soup one, on the kitchen table, under the salt? The message had never been for Nell. She had imagined a woman who had given up; the woman was a mother who had run out of time, saying at last what the archive had been for.

There's nothing left here to keep — meaning the town, only the town. I'm coming home — meaning I will come to you. Don't wait up — meaning it's all right. It had always been.

That evening Nell carried the kitchen recorder to the line. Salt had flowered across its battery contacts — a white fern, a small patient coast. She cleaned it with the toothbrush, then stopped, put the toothbrush down, and left the rest. Let it keep; it had kept the cassette safe for ten years, wherever that had been.

She played her own voice: "I made soup, the kind you liked, with the little noodles." The long pause. Then she spoke over it: "And I'm coming. Not next spring. Now. The soup's in the freezer; I'll bring the noodles."

She sent it. The machine clicked, the same click as the reel. On the display, between one blink and the next, the salt finished drawing the shoreline. She let it stay.

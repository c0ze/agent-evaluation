# The Salt Index

The third box weighed more than the first four put together, and the reason was the batteries. Every tape in it was some relative's idea of a keepsake: a phone left face-down in a drawer for six years, a dictation pen bought for shopping lists and used once for a voicemail nobody sent. Ilse had numbered the contents to three thousand one hundred and forty-one. Now the box sat on her knee in the dry room of the old Coastguard station, too heavy to open on her lap.

The station had been built to get messages off the coast. Now it held the ones that had never gone.

The recorder sat on the table with salt in its grille. She knew better than to wipe it. She had wiped it twice a season for eleven years, and every time the holes came back furred. So she didn't wipe it. She pressed play. Salt never stopped the playback, it only muffled it, and a muffled voice from 2009 was still a voice, and a voice from 2009 was the only thing in this building that had chosen to exist.

Three thousand one hundred and forty-two. A man rehearsing an apology to his brother. He got as far as the name and laughed at himself, and then kept going, and the going was the good part, the apology buried under three false starts and eleven seconds of kitchen.

Three thousand one hundred and forty-three. Nothing. Nine seconds of a fridge.

Ilse wrote the durations on the sheet. The dehumidifier in the corner, which had cost the council four hundred pounds, gave off a smell like a wet dog and had been dying for eleven years. Outside the glass the water was at the second step, which everybody agreed was nothing, and which Ilse had begun to find restful the way you find restful a tap.

Then the index ran out.

The index was the one part of the job that lied. Every message was catalogued; the index was where she wrote what a message was *about*, in her own words, which were always wrong in the same direction. So she went to the index, and there was one line in it more than there were messages in the building.

One more file. Three thousand one hundred and forty-five. Dated the eleventh of October, eleven years out, on a drive sealed since March. Eight minutes and four seconds, and it began with a click she knew in her teeth: the recorder's own start-click, flat since the battery contacts corroded.

She should have called someone. Instead she sat with her hand flat on the box and let it play.

*Ilse.* The voice was hers, hoarser, with something happening on the left side of the mouth that her mouth had never done. *I know what you're thinking. I know which box you're holding. I know it's Tuesday, because you'll have put the kettle on and I am sorry it is Tuesday.*

Ilse did not move. Behind the wall the sea worked at the pilings with the patience of something paid by the hour.

*The water's at the second step,* the voice said, and Ilse heard it as a lament, as a report from inside a room like this one that was slowly becoming a boat. *It came up to the fourth in the third winter. It came up to the fourth, Ilse, and the year before that it was the landing stage. I am not warning you. There's nothing coming. Nothing ever came. It's raining, the pump's holding, the till works.*

She stopped it there. Her hand was not steady, and she had four hundred and eleven things to do before the light went, and none of them were this.

Then she ran it back and listened properly, which is a different act.

It was a receipt. Every sentence was an inventory, kept for eleven years by somebody who had learned the sound of her from listening. The water was at the second step, which was a fact and not a lament. The pumps were holding, and holding *again*, and that was the sentence. Not *it came up to the fourth*. *It came up to the fourth and then it stopped coming up.*

The file was corrupt. Ilse knew it was corrupt, because a corrupter plays differently. It had gaps, and the gaps were where the woman had not been able to speak for a moment.

*Now the part you'll get wrong.* A cough she did not have. *The salt. I cleaned the equipment. I took the crust off the ports in January, so: the salt took the ports out in the third winter. Then I read about a man in a lifeboat who lost his radio to the sea and found it working, because the sea had grown him a seal. It grew him a seal, Ilse. So I stopped. I never cleaned anything again. It's the only job in the world that gets better if you leave it alone long enough.*

Ilse took her hand off the box.

*And one more thing.* *I got the voice right. I got it right. It's a good voice for reading lists of things people didn't say. Use it. And don't you dare throw that box in the water. The salt will get into the seams and close them, and the box will float, and whoever is here in ten years will find it and be furious.*

The click. Then the flat hiss of a room with nobody in it.

Ilse sat for a while, the sheet under her hand with the last line blank. Salt had climbed the window frames in fine white branching. The concrete under the reading desk had gone granular, the floor becoming beach again. She had been standing on a beach for eleven years and calling it a floor, and did not mind. It was the only thing in the room that had never needed her to be right.

She had not thrown anything in the water. She looked at the tape in her hand, made by a woman who had learned her voice from her tapes: the pump was holding, and it was holding again, and that was worth telling somebody, and she was somebody.

Ilse wrote it on the line. Then she sealed the box, wrote the date on the tape, and set it on the shelf with the others, where it would be found, in a building that kept its records by growing over them.

Outside, the water was at the second step.

# A Way Home

Salt had grown a white eyebrow above the tablet's charging port. Nell scraped it with a wooden toothpick, holding the socket downward so the crystals fell onto her skirt. Across the library floor, the removal men had left pale rectangles where the shelves had stood. Her table occupied the rectangle marked LOCAL HISTORY.

The last collection was a biscuit tin of phones. Their owners had surrendered them with handwritten permissions: recordings only, no photographs, no contacts. Nell kept those slips under a stone. At four, the town's electricity would be disconnected. At five, the causeway would close permanently.

She connected the next phone through an adapter, recording its audio into the tablet. An unsent message opened halfway through a breath.

“Dad, the thing about the money—”

A gull cried behind the speaker. Then silence, then a thumb rubbing the microphone. Nell saved the original, copied it, entered the date. She never finished anybody's sentence in the catalogue.

Her own phone vibrated against the tin.

*I'm outside. We need to go*, wrote Evie.

Nell typed *Ten minutes*. Yesterday she had typed the same thing, and her daughter had waited forty.

Evie's car appeared between the boards over the window. A small blue car, its boot packed to the glass. Nell's suitcase was in there. Evie had packed it without asking, rolling the sleeves inside the dresses the way Nell had taught her.

She put her phone facedown.

The next handset belonged to Mrs Vale, who had moved inland on Tuesday. It held seventeen recordings addressed to a sister who lived three streets away. Invitations, mostly. One complaint about a borrowed casserole dish. Nell labelled the folder and opened its final file.

Her own voice said, “Don't come back for me. I'll find my own way.”

She took off the headphones.

Beyond the window, Evie had lowered her forehead onto the steering wheel. Nell watched until she lifted it again.

The file was dated 18 October 2054. Today was 18 October 2044. Mrs Vale's other recordings had ordinary dates. Nell checked the handset's clock, then the raw timestamp. She unplugged it, cleaned a crust of salt from the adapter, and tried again.

“Don't come back for me. I'll find my own way.”

The voice had Nell's small hitch on *find*, left over from childhood speech lessons. But it was thinner, roughened at the edges. In the background something rattled, followed by a long soft hiss.

She listened a third time. The woman sounded tired. Nell touched her throat.

Her mother had refused the first relocation offer. By the time she accepted the second, seawater came up through her kitchen drain. Nell had carried the sewing machine upstairs, then the kitchen chairs, then a drawer full of tangled cables. Her mother had stood at the stair foot saying that none of it could be left, though she could no longer remember what most of the cables fitted.

Now Nell imagined her own older body in a room above water. Evie driving back along some remaining road. That voice telling her, at last, to leave.

She copied the file into her personal folder.

There were eleven phones still in the tin. She calculated their transfer times. If she skipped the cataloguing, if she used both adapters—

Evie knocked on the boarded window.

Nell raised one finger. She heard herself through the headphones again, although playback had stopped.

She stood, unplugged the drives and packed the phones in their tin. They could be catalogued somewhere else. At the foot of the table lay a cardboard box of chargers the council had sent her. She left it there.

Evie came in while Nell was fastening the bag.

“Ready?”

“Yes.”

Her daughter looked behind her, searching for the qualification.

Nell lifted the bag herself. The tin knocked against her hip. By the door, the returns machine displayed PLEASE INSERT ITEM, though its slot had been sealed for a month.

“Still taking complaints,” Nell said.

Evie gave a single surprised laugh.

Outside, Nell put her hand on the car roof to steady herself. Salt had flowered in the seam around the aerial. Evie waited beside the open passenger door. Nell got in.

Ten years later, Nell could manage the walk to the harbour if she stopped at the bakery. The bench there was the right height for her knee, and the baker would bring her tea in a cup instead of cardboard.

Evie had driven her that morning. Now she stood beside the car, reading a message from work. Nell could see her forming a new arrangement: the harbour, then home, then the delayed appointment. Evie had been making such arrangements for years. Nell had let her.

“Go,” Nell said. “I'll have my tea.”

“I'll be twenty minutes.”

Before Nell could answer, Evie was in the car.

The bench was damp. Nell wiped it with her sleeve and sat. She brought out her phone to tell Evie she needn't return. Typing hurt her thumbs now, so she pressed the microphone.

“Don't come back for me. I'll find my own way.”

Behind her, the baker rattled the cups into their rack. The milk steamer hissed.

Nell kept her thumb on the screen. She had played the old file often in the first year inland, then less often. Its silence had always seemed enormous. Now she could hear a spoon being laid on a saucer.

Evie's car was waiting at the junction. Nell cancelled the recording, rose and waved. When Evie lowered the window, she said it again, loudly enough to carry.

Her daughter hesitated, then waved back and turned.

Nell sat down. Salt had begun to crust her phone's speaker grille. She brushed it away and put the phone in her bag. When the tea arrived, she curled both hands around the cup.

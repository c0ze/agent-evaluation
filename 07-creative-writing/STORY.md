# The Third Stair

The salt came out of the charging port in one piece, a white splinter the exact shape of the hole it had filled. Tamsin Rowe laid it on the blotter beside the others. By the end of a day there was always a row of them, like the teeth of something small.

She worked on the upper floor of the Polwidden customs house. The ground floor took water on the spring tides now, and the council had stopped sending anyone to pump it. Their contract with her was specific. Sent messages lived on servers inland and would outlast everybody. What the Coastal Record wanted was the other kind: voice messages recorded and never sent, which existed only in the handsets, in drawers and coat pockets, in a town that had until March to be empty.

So: a soft brush, isopropyl, two clips from the bench supply, and patience while the storage woke. Copy the drafts folder. Listen to each file once, all the way through. Fill in the form. *Speaker. Intended recipient. Date. Disposition of device.* She had done four hundred and eleven. Apologies, mostly. Instructions for boilers. A man explaining to a daughter in Perth, over six minutes, that he was not going to explain himself.

Today's crate had come from the chapel porch, where a recycling box had stood for eleven years under a laminated sign promising that old phones would be sent to those in need. They had been sent, in the end, about forty yards.

The nineteenth handset was a black slab with crazed glass and salt furred along its seams like frost on a car door. It held one draft. The date on it was ten years from the coming October, which she noted without interest. With no network to correct it, a handset believes its own clock, and a clock is a sliver of quartz and whatever has grown on it. She had catalogued messages from 1970 and from the year 2106.

She put the headphones on.

"It's me."

It was. Not the voice she heard in her head but the one recordings gave back when she tested levels: flat, slightly nasal, with this coast in the r's. Lower, though. Rubbed down. If she'd had to catalogue it she would have put ten years on it.

"Water's at the third stair and holding. I've the kettle and the radio up top and I'm all right. So don't come. Hear me? There's nothing here wants saving. You stay where you are."

Then eleven seconds of room: a radio murmuring, something knocking, wood on wood. Then nothing.

Tamsin took the headphones off and set them on the blotter, square to its edge. She looked at the date again and it had not changed.

She lived at 6 Chapel Row, in a house with thirteen stairs, and the relocation form had been on her kitchen table under the fruit bowl since June. Everybody else on the Row had signed.

So that was how it went. She stayed. In ten years the water was at the third stair and she was up top with the kettle, sounding fine about it, telling somebody not to come. She tried to work out who the somebody was. It didn't take long to go through the list.

She played it four more times. On the form she typed the date as given and left *Speaker* empty, which she had never done.

She walked home by the top road with the handset in her coat pocket, which was against her own procedure. In the hall she stood at the foot of the stairs, feeling foolish, and counted.

One. Two. Three.

Beside the third stair, level as a ruled line, ran a mark on the wallpaper that she had stopped seeing years ago: a brown edge, and above it a faint glittering band where salt had come up through the paper and set. She had painted over it the first spring. The salt had come through the paint.

On the landing windowsill sat her mother's radio. She picked it up and tried the battery door with her thumbnail. It was crusted shut, white along the join.

The water had already been at the third stair. Ten Octobers back, in the surge, while Tamsin sat in a flat in Exeter with the roads closed at Launceston, telling herself the roads were closed.

*It's me.* Only one person had ever begun a call to her that way, as if there were nobody else it could be. And people used to say that on the phone they couldn't tell the two of them apart. She had let herself forget that, because the voice she'd kept was the last one, thin, from a high bed in Truro two winters after. Her mother had been sixty-one that October. Tamsin did the subtraction and didn't like how evenly it came out.

She sat down on the third stair.

*Don't come.* She hadn't. For ten years that had been the fact about her, the one she'd moved back here to live beside, and her mother had never raised it. Her mother had said the hall carpet was a write-off and that the Pascoes had lost their freezer.

And all that time this had been in a box in the chapel porch. Failed sends went to a different folder; Tamsin knew the difference better than anyone living. This had been recorded, and kept, and not sent. She had four hundred and eleven of those and a rough idea of why people made them. Some said too much. Some were true only while the recording ran. Some were the thing you wanted to have said, in case the other person turned up anyway.

She could make it mean either. She tried both, sitting there, with the handset in her lap leaving white on her skirt.

*There's nothing here wants saving.* That part only went one way.

In the morning she opened the record and filled in the empty field. *Speaker: Rowe, Loveday. Intended recipient: daughter.* She corrected the date. Under *Disposition of device* she typed *returned to family*, and signed the release twice, once on each line.

That evening she lifted the fruit bowl. The form underneath had kept its shape, a pale ring on the paper. *Property address* was easy. At *Forwarding address* she stopped with the pen raised, and on the table beside her the phone went on quietly growing its salt.

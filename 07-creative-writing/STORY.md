# The Weigh Office

Nell took the back off Mr. Venn’s handset and found a ridge of salt where the battery should have met the contacts. Under the bench lamp the crystals had the square, stepped faces of the houses on the hill.

“It said waterproof on the box,” Mr. Venn said. “The box is fine.”

She loosened the ridge with a brush and clipped the reader’s lead to the handset’s service pins. It gave up seven drafts. Six were about the flood, dated from the night the siren failed. In one, Mr. Venn stood in his kitchen describing the water coming up through the screw holes in the floor. He wanted those six in the town archive. For the seventh he asked Nell to turn the monitor so he could see the little bars that meant his voice was still there. He let her play the first few seconds to check the transfer.

“June, I’ve got your side of the bed level. It was that folded bit of newspaper all along.”

He touched the top edge of the monitor. Nell stopped the playback. June had been dead since before the sirens were installed.

“Can I have that one to take with me?”

Nell said she’d find him something that would play it. He went down to the quay to see about a seat.

The launch’s wake had come under the door of the old weigh office twice that week. At four the launch would make its last trip. Nell had packed the reader and the archive drives as soon as she’d digitized the last cassette. A woman from the flats had brought it in a padded envelope with her brother’s address in blue ink and no stamp. Nell had to unbuckle the case again when Mr. Venn knocked. Her own bag was under the bench. In its side pocket was a sandwich wrapped in the notice giving the date after which her street address would cease to exist.

The reader chimed. Eight drafts, it said.

She opened the extra one, expecting another strip of sound from Mr. Venn’s kitchen. The date was November 18, 2051. Exactly ten years ahead. Her own name was in the sender field. There was a scrape like a cuff being drawn across a table, then a woman spoke.

“Leave me where you found me.”

At the start of *where* there was the little whistle that came from the gap between Nell’s front teeth. She put her tongue there. The woman took two breaths where Nell would have taken one, but there was also that peculiar lift at the end, the one her sister used to mimic when Nell insisted she had made up her mind.

She unclipped the lead from Mr. Venn’s handset. The draft stayed. She checked the reader’s clock against the one above the door and found the usual forty seconds between them. The source field gave the reader’s own serial number. She tipped the machine up to check, and a few grains of salt came free onto her jeans.

She wanted to hear the cuff scrape again. Her sleeve made much the same sound on the bench. She could imagine coming back to this room and finding it dried out, the lamp cord draped over the nail where it belonged. She had carried her house key in her fist all the way down the hill that morning. She took it from her pocket now and laid it beside the reader. *Leave me where you found me.* If the woman came back here in ten years, Nell wanted her to find the key.

Mr. Venn came back with a clean handset in a padded sleeve. His daughter had sent it across with the launch pilot. Nell started to show him how to play a saved file. He tried twice to get a word in.

“Will it open the way it used to?”

She clipped the reader’s lead back on and asked him to show her. On the old handset, he tapped the blank space under June’s name and held his finger there. A little microphone appeared. Below it was a list of dates. He chose one and the bars came up again, with the new recording button still within reach of his thumb.

“I might have more to tell her,” he said.

The bench shifted as somebody rolled a hand truck down the quay. Nell steadied both handsets. On the new one she made a contact for June, leaving the number empty, and copied the draft into it. Mr. Venn held the speaker close to his ear. She could hear a little burst of breath and then his thumb came down. He started the message over.

While he listened, Nell took another look at the draft from 2051. The heading gave her name in full. She had supplied it herself when she set up the reader, so the people who brought in their phones would know whose machine they were agreeing to trust. The space for a recipient was empty. She had been sitting there all this time feeling addressed. Now, when she played it once more, she heard how low the woman was speaking. She’d used that voice herself to try out words she didn’t yet want anyone to answer.

Nell sent the six flood recordings to the archive drives and waited for both copies to verify. She ejected the small card that held the reader’s own drafts. It fit in the side pocket beside her sandwich. She left the house key on the bench.

On the launch Mr. Venn took the window seat. Nell sat beside him with the case between her boots. Along the bottom of the window, salt had begun to fur the screws of the defroster. Mr. Venn cupped the new handset under his chin. His thumb found the microphone, and Nell bent to tighten the straps on the case while he told June who was taking him home.

# Clean Handover

The salt came off the circuit board in flakes, like the skin of a healed burn, and Mira caught them in a saucer because the protocol said debris had to be weighed and logged, and the protocol was the only thing in Carrow still running at full strength.

She worked out of the old music room. The piano was still against the wall, its keys furred white with salt that came back overnight — something patient, working out from the wood. Through the floor vent she could hear the sea in the storm drain: not loud, just steady, a man in another room refusing to leave.

She was an archivist by training and a residue technician by contract. When the decommissioning order came down, the authority found that people leave behind things that are not objects — drafts, voice memos spoken into a phone at two in the morning, saved, never sent. The authority called it residue, and it had to be preserved before the water took it. So Mira digitized each one, played the first eight seconds, typed a tag, and wiped the device for a clean handover. Nothing after eight seconds was her business.

Box 14 came from her mother's house on Fenwick Street. She had packed it herself and had not opened it since. Inside was the cordless handset her mother kept by the good chair, and a phone with a crack across the screen shaped like a river delta. That one had been Mira's, at seventeen.

The handset's charging pins were furred with salt, and it took a charge anyway. Then the index: eleven drafts from her mother's number, all in one week in April, and at the bottom a twelfth file dated ten years from Tuesday.

She should have logged it. She put in the earbuds.

It was her own voice. Older — a rasp in it, the way her grandmother's had gone at the end — and slower, as though the speaker had learned to be careful with air. Behind it, gulls, and a rope knocking against a mast.

"Mira. I'll say this fast." A breath. "Don't do the last box. Do the rest. But the box with the cassettes — leave it. And the chair." A pause long enough that she checked the counter. "I should have let you keep the chair. I've thought about it for years, which is a stupid thing to spend years on. But there it is." Then, quieter: "Put salt on the sill. Coarse, not fine."

The sound of a handset being set down, carefully, by someone who had done it a thousand times.

She played it again. The date held. The phone had been sealed in a box for two years; the file had a checksum, and the timestamp was 2036. Mira was not a woman given to explanations, and not a woman who could leave a sentence unfinished.

She decided it was a warning. Something in the finishing of it — a fire, a stair giving way. And the chair, the one she sat in every day. She dragged the stool over from the bench and worked the afternoon with her knees up around her ears.

Denny noticed at nine the next morning.

"You're not sitting in the chair," he said.

"It creaks," she said.

"It's a chair," he said. "That's the whole thing chairs do."

The weeks narrowed.

Water in the basements went from something you stepped around to something you planned for. Mira finished the F's and the H's and watched the crates go out on the Thursday ferry. She did not open box 14. She began to hear the voice at odd hours — the flat way it had said *years*, as if the regret were a piece of furniture it had learned to walk around.

The men came with the van on the last day, and she went to the house.

Fenwick Street was a canal by then, ankle-deep, with a tide line of weed across the doors. Her mother's chair was gone because she had taken it. Box 14 was in the front room, taped LAST in her own hand.

She cut the tape. Forty-one cassettes, labels in her mother's small backward-slanting hand — dates, and sometimes a word. *Rain. Bread. The Neighbors.* She found the player in the kitchen drawer and put in the earliest tape.

Her mother's voice, mid-sentence, as if she had been talking before the tape began: "— and I said to Margaret, if the man wants to park his van on the pavement then he can pay for the pavement, I'm not the council." A clatter. "Mira, if you're hearing this, the boiler man is coming Thursday and you're to let him in and not be clever at him."

Then nothing. The tape ran on, hissing, to the end of the side.

She sat on the floor of the empty house and listened. Then she understood, the way you understand a joke a day late — all at once, and in the chest.

The voice on the handset had done the last box. Of course it had. It had her patience and her forms, and the archive had kept them all, perfect and checksummed, and she had never once played them. That was the warning. Not a fire. Not the sea. A woman at a bench, being excellent, for years.

*Don't do the last box.* Not because the box was dangerous. Because doing it was how you lost it.

And the chair. In the other version she had let it go. She could hear the praise — *you're being so good about this* — and she had believed it. Not a warning either. The only apology she had.

She looked at the sill. The paint had bubbled. A line of salt lay along it already, fine as talc, blown in through the crack — the sea's version, patient, working its way toward the wires.

She carried the box out to the van and did not sign it over. The man with the clipboard asked what it was. "Residue," she said, and he wrote it down, and that was the last clean handover of her career.

That night she sat in her mother's chair, which creaked, the way chairs do, and played the tapes one after another — the player ate two — and typed nothing into any form. Before bed she poured coarse salt along the windowsill of the music room, over the fine grey drift the sea had left, and did not wipe it away.

# Intertidal

The phone came in a bread bag with a rubber band around it, and June Marsh knew before she opened it what she would find. Salt had bloomed from the charging port in a white fern, crusted the speaker mesh, powdered the seam of the case like frost on a window. She worked the crystals loose with a dental pick, the way the county manual said not to, and set the phone on the heating pad to coax it back to life.

The ferry terminal had been decommissioned for three years, which made it, by the county's logic, the driest public building in St. Armand — the waiting room sat eleven feet above the old tide line. June had chalked the new one herself behind the ticket counter; the March line was already four inches under water. She had stopped chalking. No one left wanted the information.

Forty-one phones in numbered bags. Twelve answering machines. A shoebox of MiniDV tapes that smelled of vinegar. The county called it the Departure Archive: residents taking the buyout could leave their unsent messages — the drafts, the deleted voicemails, the recordings made at three in the morning and never played — to be digitized before the town was surrendered. In the state's folders St. Armand was no longer "condemned" but "intertidal," a word June privately admired for its courtesy. It made drowning sound like a zoning condition.

She found the file on a Tuesday, at the end of the dub queue on her own field recorder. Forty seconds long. Stamped with a date ten years and two months into a future the county had already pronounced drowned.

Clocks drifted, she told herself. Batteries died and little brains forgot the century.

She pressed play because not pressing play would have meant admitting what she already knew about the voice.

It was hers. Older. Rougher, with salt in it — and the part of her mind that filled in catalog fields wrote *source: J. Marsh; recipient: J. Marsh; relationship: estranged.*

"File this wherever you keep the outtakes. It's June. You'll know the voice. The house held — the water stops at the third step, and some nights it goes back, I don't know why, I've stopped asking. Two things. You were right about the outtakes: unsent isn't the same as unsaid. Say that back to yourself until it's true. And play yours now, June. Not last. Now. Don't save everything. She's still listening."

Then static, and under it a sound that took four listens to name: gulls, and the buoy bell off the drowned pier — closer than it could be from the terminal. As if recorded from a porch.

She played it standing, then sitting, then with her forehead against the cool metal of the dubbing deck. *The house held.* That was the first reading, the greedy one: she survived, the house survived, staying was permitted. Ten years out, a June with salt in her voice had not been punished for refusing the ferries. *Play yours now.* An order. Her future self had turned out brisk, like a nurse.

Her own unsent message lived in the bread bag at the bottom of her desk drawer, on the phone she had never handed in, though she was the entire receiving department. Eleven seconds, addressed to Mara at a number that was a billing notice now. June had carried it through three winters of evacuations the way other people carry a stone in a coat pocket: to know the weight was still there.

She did not play it. She filed the future file under MARSH, J. — ANOMALOUS, and went home across the footbridge; in the morning there was salt on the recorder's microphone holes, a fine white stitching, though the recorder had never left the dehumidified room. She wiped it with her shirttail. It was back the next morning, and the one after, and she understood the salt was not on the machine but in it, precipitating out of forty seconds of audio the way it precipitated out of everything, given time.

On the ninth night she locked the terminal door and played her own message aloud into the empty waiting room.

*Mara. It's June. The water's past the post office. I'm not—* A long pause, the small weather of breathing. *Come home.*

That was all — the thing she had never sent: the asking. She had let Mara go on the last ferry with a casserole dish and a kiss on the forehead, both of them performing calm like actors who had rehearsed the wrong play, and the eleven seconds stayed in the drawer because sending them would have meant admitting which of them had been wrong to go.

The echo came back off the ferry schedule board, and June stood still inside it.

*Unsent isn't the same as unsaid.* She had heard it as comfort — the archive as absolution, all those drafts doing their work in storage. Not that. The saying was the act; the delivery never had been. Her future self hadn't been describing the archive; she had been correcting its premise. And *she's still listening* — June had spent nine nights deciding whether that was hope or cruelty, whether it meant Mara had kept the number somehow, somewhere dry. It meant neither. The listener was here. The listener had been here all along, in the waiting room, keeping four dozen strangers' unsaid things eleven feet above the tide. The message from the future was not a promise that she survived. It was a description of the job, handed down like a set of keys.

She played the future file once more. *Don't save everything.* First hearing: permission to triage, to let some of the bread bags go to the incinerator. Now she heard the whole of it — you could not shelve a town and call that keeping it. Save the thing that says back. Let the rest be sea.

June took her field recorder off the dock, held it at arm's length the way she had seen residents do, shy as brides. She recorded eleven seconds, then forty, then stopped counting. She labeled the file MARSH, J. — OUTTAKE, and dated it ten years from now, because it had never occurred to her that a timestamp could be an appointment, and someone would have to keep it.

On the microphone's black foam a single crystal of salt caught the desk lamp and held it, the way frost holds morning. She left it there. It was the first thing in the archive that had ever grown.

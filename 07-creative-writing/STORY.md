# Salt Bloom

The phones came in on Tuesdays, in shoeboxes and grocery bags, once in a bread maker. June carried them to the library's back room, set them in the dehumidifier's breath, and took off the voice memos: the unsent ones. Cormorant Key was leaving in stages, and everyone had recordings they'd never said out loud — to dead husbands, to estranged children, to the town itself. Four hundred eleven by October, cataloged the way she'd been trained. The county's buyout paperwork called the project a cultural asset. The town's assets, as far as June could determine, were three jet skis, a bait freezer, and a decorative lighthouse that had never once been lit. She listed them and billed the state.

Salt got into everything: bloomed over charging ports like frost, crept green-white along the seams of every board, filmed the contacts she cleaned nightly with a toothbrush and isopropyl. Salt preserved and salt corroded; the ocean had never much cared which. By the second year of the buyout she could date a phone by the crust on its screws.

The anomalous handset arrived wrapped in a dish towel, left in the transfer station's donation bin by someone no one could name. Slate-gray, no SIM, swollen where water had gotten in. Its lock screen read October 14, 2036.

*Clock battery*, she wrote on the intake form. Then she charged it. One unsent message, and the voice was hers. She knew voices the way other people knew faces — this one ten years heavier, with the catch on the r's she'd had since childhood.

"It's June," the message said. "Before you go looking, it isn't the battery. I've checked it every year for ten years. The water reaches the depot steps with the November king tide. The drives on the third shelf hold. I still open the archive on Wednesdays. Even the ones I can't answer. Especially hers. I finally listened, and it doesn't ruin you. That's what I need you to know. It doesn't ruin you. The salt gets into everything. The drives still spin."

One minute and six seconds. She played it three times, then looked at her desk drawer — the only file in the building she had never opened: `0023-mhalloway-unsent.wav`. Her mother had left it at the station three years ago, before going inland to die without, true to form, saying goodbye in person. June had cataloged it herself. She had never played it.

She bagged the phone, labeled it *ANOMALOUS — CLOCK FAULT*, and didn't sleep much that month.

The king tide came over the seawall at three in the morning, November fourth. June watched from the co-op door in waders as the water climbed the depot steps to the third and stopped, lapping, as though it had read the message too. The drives were already on the third shelf. She had moved them up in October and had not examined why.

Tomás drove the last county shuttle. There was a desk for her in the county seat; she said she'd be on the next one. They both knew what that meant. He shook her hand, palm gritty with the same salt, and the shuttle went out past the decorative lighthouse and didn't come back.

On the first Wednesday after, she took out the drawer file: three minutes of waveform, a thin start, a thick middle, almost nothing at the end. She pressed play.

"June, it's Mom. The station was — I'm not good at that. Standing there with the bags. What I should have said: it isn't the house, it isn't the town. I stayed angry at your father so long I forgot to practice saying anything else. So this is me practicing. I kept every one of your cassettes — the field recordings, the gull ones — the ones you thought I threw out. That's most of the message. The water — they say it's everywhere now. Take care of your hands."

The file ended. The dehumidifier hummed. June looked at her hands — chapped from the alcohol, nails cut short for the toothbrush work — and sat with them in the blue light. Below the street, the sea went on.

In the morning she played the 2036 message again. It hadn't changed: one minute and six seconds, salt blooming around its charging port.

In October she had heard a warning — a woman stranded in a drowned town, keeping the dead company on Wednesdays, promising that listening to her mother wouldn't ruin her, as if surviving were the most anyone could hope for. The same sentences landed differently now. *I still open the archive* came through as a practice, not a sentence. *Especially hers*, a door left unlocked and then walked through. And *it doesn't ruin you* had the sound of something a person says only after it has failed to.

Beneath it all lay her first error: she had cataloged the message unsent, like the others — a draft from a broken clock. But drafts don't cross ten years of brine to arrive in a donation bin. Every row in the database said UNSENT. This one had been delivered — past the calendar, past the crust, into the only hands it could reach.

She found the row and replaced the label with a single word: SENT. The only one in four hundred twelve.

That night she opened the recorder on her own phone. Salt had crusted the port white as frost; she scraped it away with a thumbnail, because a contact has to be clean, because anything you mean to keep you keep cleaning. She pressed record.

"It's June," she said. "It isn't the battery. The drives still spin — I check them every Wednesday, same as you're checking now. The water reaches the depot steps, the third shelf holds, and you will know why before you'll admit it. Your mother ends with your hands. Look at them. Listen to it anyway. It doesn't ruin you." Surf lapped past the seawall. She let it stay in the recording. "The salt gets into everything. Some of it is preserving. I'm learning to tell the difference. That's what I need you to know."

She didn't save it as a draft. She addressed it to her own number and pressed send, and the little arrow climbed the screen, the same as for birthday cards and grocery lists. Out on the street the water was going down. It was Wednesday; the archive was open; under the crust she cleaned nightly, the contact held.

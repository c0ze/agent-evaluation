# Efflorescence

Each morning there was more salt than the night before. Ilse Varga found it first on the scanner glass, a fine white bloom in the corners of the platen, then on the brass of the drive ports, and once inside the battery door of a dead man's pocket recorder, where it had crystallized into a small, perfect fern. She cleared it with a cocktail stick and a square of cloth she kept folded in her shirt pocket. The silica packs beside the monitor swelled like cheeks. She replaced them every afternoon. Nothing she did changed the rate.

The Annex had been the Harbor Credit Union until the county bought it and stopped paying for heat. The intake bins still sat in the old vault, gray crates labeled by street. On the wall beside the door someone had penciled the high water each morning in short tally marks. The last was August. Beside it, in capitals, someone had written NO and gone over it until the paper tore.

The county called it managed retreat. The program was called Voices, Unsent, and its flyer showed a gull holding a phone. Residents brought in whatever they had: drafts never sent, pocket recorders, voicemail exports from carriers closing in the spring. They signed a form. Ilse copied each file onto two drives, logged it, and sealed the original in a gray bag for the boat. She was not supposed to listen. She listened to the first two seconds, the way you test bathwater with a wrist, and then she listened to the rest.

Walt Fenn's submission ran fourteen seconds: a throat cleared, "Right, then," a long breath out, and silence. Under *Reason for archival* he had written *Vanity, mostly*. She gave him the highest fidelity the software allowed, which preserved, with perfect accuracy, the gravel in his voice that he had spent sixty years successfully ignoring.

Her own unsent messages lived in a folder on her phone: forty-one drafts to Mattias, her son in the city, each opening with the same three words. *It's not that.* She recorded them in the car behind the Annex with the heater running and deleted them before the second sentence. He rang on Sundays. She let it ring out, then played his voicemail twice in the dark to be sure it had not changed.

The file arrived on a Thursday. It did not come from a resident. It came from the handset she used as a field recorder, the one she had bought for the program and never used for anything else. Its metadata dated the recording 11 October 2036, 06:14. Ten years, to the day. The source field read *this device*.

The clock chip in that handset had been crusted with salt since August. She had cleaned its contacts twice. She told herself the date was the chip's fault, put in her earbuds, and pressed play.

Her voice, but lower, with the roughness at the back of it that came when she was tired, and the flattened vowel she lost on her own name.

"Ilse. Don't go back for the north drives. I know you're going to. The bins will be standing in water by four, and the stairs go under before the slip does. You'll be on the third step when it reaches you, and you won't hear the door. Leave the blue case on the sill. The county can have the rest. Don't play this for Dev. He'll file it with the others, and it isn't for the others."

She stopped it there. Outside, the tide had reached the second bollard early, a bright skin spreading over the stones. She put the handset in the drawer with the silica and went back to work, because it was ten past two, four hundred files still needed checksums, and the alternative was to believe a clock.

Dev came at half past three in the county launch, which nosed against the slip with the caution of a man who did not trust boats. He took the bags without opening them and asked whether there was anything she needed him to know.

"Nothing you need," she said.

He nodded and carried the bags down. When the launch had cleared the breakwater, she took the handset out and played the file from the beginning, past the bins and the stairs and the blue case, into the part she had not let herself hear.

"I'm not angry that you kept them. You kept everybody's. Walt's going to be fine. Everybody's going to be fine who wasn't waiting on you. Take the four-ten. There's always room on the four-ten, and nobody's counting you on it.

"I went back for the drives every time. Ten years of going back.

"Ring him on a Sunday. Don't do the voice, you'll hate the voice. He picks up on the third ring, if you say his name first."

*Don't go back* had sounded like a warning about water. Now it sounded like someone who had been going back for a decade and was tired of the stairs.

The four-ten was twenty minutes out. She went down to the vault, found the blue case where it had sat since August, and carried it up to the window. She set it on the sill, as instructed. Salt had already begun on its clasps, a fringe of crystals like sugar on the rim of a plate. She did not go back for the north drives. She put on her coat.

On the stairs she counted the steps out of habit. The third was still dry.

The water was lifting weed against the bottom plank of the slip. The four-ten came in low and settled in the swell, and a young deckhand with a county lanyard held out his hand. She took it. On the bench at the stern, the handset in her lap, she saw that the speaker grille had gone white at the edges. She opened the folder. Forty-one drafts, each a sentence she had never finished.

She did not open one. She scrolled past them to his name, the one she had only ever let ring out, and pressed it.

It rang once, and again. On the third ring she heard him breathe in to say hello, and before he could, she said his name.

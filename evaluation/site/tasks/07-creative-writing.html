Twenty Minutes Fast

The salt came off the battery contacts in white scabs, and Ines worked it loose with a toothbrush dipped in isopropyl, the way she had worked loose the last four hundred. Under the crust the brass had gone green. She dabbed it dry, fitted two new AAs, and the little recorder lit its display like something waking in a ward.

The crate beside her desk held the rest of Harrow Point's afternoon: a cordless phone with a cracked cradle, three handsets from the era of sliding keyboards, a child's walkie-talkie shaped like a frog. Each wore a luggage tag stamped in the relocation office's green ink. Unsent drafts. Resident consents to archive. People brought them in on their way out of town and set them on the desk without quite letting go, as if handing over a key.

The reading room had been emptied of shelves in April. What remained was her desk, the scanner, the audio interface, and the dehumidifier the county had sent in May, which bore a yellow sticker advising that it not be operated in damp environments. She ran it anyway. It filled a bucket every six hours, and each time she carried the bucket to the sink she felt she was doing a small, precise amount of good.

Salt got into everything. It furred the headphone jack of her laptop. It bloomed on the USB ports in tiny cubes, perfect under the loupe, as if the sea were practising architecture. She wiped the ports every morning with a lint-free cloth, and every afternoon the cubes were back.

The recorder held one file. The display gave its date as the fourteenth of March, ten years from now.

She wrote clock fault? in the log and pressed play.

"It's me."

Ines took her hand off the trackpad.

The voice was hers. Not like hers: hers, with ten more years in it, a rasp at the bottom where the vowels had worn thin. Even the breath before the first word was hers, the small intake she heard on her own voicemail greeting and had never managed to edit out.

"I'm down at the wall by the chandlery. Where the steps were. It's past the fourth rail now." A gull, close. Wind rubbing the microphone. "I kept all of them, love. Every one. I thought if I kept them you'd—" A gap, long enough that Ines glanced at the waveform to see if the file had ended. "Anyway. You don't have to come back for it. You can let it go under."

The file ended.

She played it again. She played it a third time with the headphones on and the dehumidifier off, and in the quiet she could hear a halyard ticking against a mast somewhere behind the voice, and under that the sea, doing what it did.

I kept all of them. She looked at the shelf of labelled drives: eleven hundred files, a town's worth of things people had almost said. Her contract ran through December. She had told the relocation officer, twice, that she would stay until the last device came in, and he had written late departure on his clipboard and underlined it.

You can let it go under.

She sat with that. It was not unreasonable, she thought, that a woman of fifty-one might stand on a wall and tell a woman of forty-one she had spent a decade on a drowned town's drafts. What was unreasonable was the gentleness. Ines was not, as a rule, gentle with herself. And love. She had never once called herself love.

She took the recorder to the window and turned it under the light. No name on the tag, only the green stamp; the drop box by the front steps took things at night. Grey plastic, a model from before the floodwall went up. A strip of white medical tape across the back. Under the tape, where the salt had not reached, there was handwriting in biro faded to violet.

Set 20 fast.

Her mother had kept every clock in the house twenty minutes fast. The oven, the bedside radio, the car. So you're never late, she'd say, and then be late anyway, because she knew about the twenty minutes and took them back.

Ines held down the menu button. The date field blinked. On this model the year came first, where anyone would expect the minutes to be, and someone had pressed up twenty times and been satisfied.

Twenty years forward of a day ten years gone.

Ten years ago Ines had been in the city. Ten years ago the phone had rung most evenings, and she had let most of it go to voicemail, because the calls were always about the house, the wall, the water, whether Ines might come home for a weekend and help decide. Everyone had said they sounded alike on the phone. The butcher on Quay Street had called Ines Mrs Barros for years, and eventually she had stopped correcting him.

Past the fourth rail. The water had reached the fourth rail of the old tide gauge in the storm year. It was over the ninth now.

She thought of her own messages from that time, brief and dutiful and slightly hurried, and of a woman on a wall with a phone full of them, deciding not to send the one thing she'd recorded back.

Ines had come home eventually. Too late for the part that mattered, and then she had stayed, and begun keeping things.

On the sill where she had set the recorder down, new cubes had already started on the speaker grille, small and white and exact.

She uploaded the file and logged it under her mother's name. Then she put on her coat, and put the recorder in the pocket, and walked down Quay Street to the chandlery, where the steps used to be. The wall was wet to the tenth rail and the halyards were ticking. She stood where she guessed her mother had stood. She counted the rails that were left, which did not take long.

She pressed record, and listened for a while to the wind in the microphone, and said, in the voice everyone had always mistaken, "It's me."

A Number for the House

Ruth picked salt from a charging socket with a bristle pulled from her own hairbrush. Under the desk lamp the crystals looked square, almost manufactured. She tipped them onto black paper before trying the cable again.

The phone belonged to the butcher. Its release slip permitted her to copy three unsent messages to his brother, but not the fourth. She obeyed. In the third he described how to dismantle the cold room, naming each bolt, although his brother had been buried inland four years earlier.

She entered refrigeration; family correspondence in the catalogue.

Through the library's uncovered windows she could see the bolts sticking out of the pavement where the benches had been removed. The final relocation bus left at six. Tomorrow the pumping station would lose its power allocation. Notices called this the return of the tidal basin. Ruth had lived in the tidal basin for fifty-three years without knowing its name.

The archive occupied two drives and eleven shoeboxes. People brought obsolete phones, pocket recorders, hearing aids with dictation buffers: things that retained words after the intention to send them had passed. Ruth played them through a converter, watching the waveform crawl across her screen. She kept the pauses.

Her own old handset waited beneath a sandwich wrapper. She had promised Eva she would process it before leaving. It held six attempts to tell her daughter that she had accepted the flat.

There were seven files.

The last was dated 18 October 2052, ten years ahead. Ruth checked the converter clock. Correct. The handset clock still believed it was March.

She put on her headphones.

“They asked for my address. I gave them this one without thinking.”

Her voice, but with something loosened in the throat. She heard the little whistle around her crooked incisor, the swallowed breath she took when an ordinary sentence became difficult.

“Then I tried to remember our house number. I had to look it up.”

A clatter behind the voice. Three blows, a space, another blow.

“I thought I ought to tell you.”

Nothing more.

She listened again, pressing the left ear cup until its cracked cushion hurt. Her father had begun with numbers. He could describe every room of the house where he had raised her, but near the end he could not have addressed an envelope to it.

Ruth wrote 26 on the converter's paper sleeve.

She could recover the voice. A number would be easy. She opened a fresh catalogue entry and began listing the house's rooms, working clockwise from the front door. In the kitchen there had been a yellow patch under the boiler where no one could reach to repaint. The bathroom catch lifted instead of turning. She ought to photograph it. There was still time if she walked quickly.

Her phone vibrated against the desk.

At the flat. Did you get on? Eva had sent a picture of a key lying beside two oranges.

Ruth typed Nearly, then deleted it.

She returned to the strange file. The clatter sounded familiar. Her father had knocked his spoon against the bedrail when he wanted someone. She searched the recording's properties for a device identifier. The field was empty.

The desk lamp dimmed, recovered. Salt had flowered along the converter's warm casing, white fur gathering at the screw heads. She brushed it away. Her thumbnail left a dark wet crescent.

In the street, a removal worker was folding the library ramp. Ruth knocked on the glass. He held up five fingers.

She looked at her list. She had reached the landing.

The butcher had left his fourth message on the phone. Whatever it contained would go back to him. She had spent the morning handling that boundary carefully, keeping her curiosity to herself.

She closed the catalogue entry without saving it.

The drives went into their waterproof case. She packed the handset separately, wrapped in her cardigan, then carried the shoeboxes out two at a time. On the last trip she remembered her sandwich. She left the paper sleeve with the number beneath the lamp.

From the bus she watched water standing in the holes where the benches had been. It was level with the paving now. She kept her eyes on one hole until the bus turned.

Eva's flat was above a pharmacy, forty kilometres inland. Ruth arrived holding the archive case across her stomach. The hall smelled of fresh plaster and somebody's fried onions. Her daughter took the case without asking and set it on a kitchen chair.

“That's your room,” Eva said.

Ruth looked towards the open door. Eva had put her hairbrush on a folded towel. One of its bristles was missing.

She wanted to say that the flat was only provisional. Instead she asked where the cups were.

As Eva reached into a cupboard, a tram passed below the window. Its wheels crossed a junction: three blows, a space, another blow.

Ruth gripped the edge of the counter.

“They stop at midnight,” Eva said. “I've checked.”

Ruth took the old handset from her cardigan. A little salt had crystallized around its speaker grille. She laid it on the counter and played the message aloud.

Eva listened with the cups in her hands.

“Is that you?”

“I think so.”

They stood through the recorded clatter. Somewhere beyond it a cupboard shut. Ruth looked at the cupboard Eva had just opened, its door hanging slightly crooked.

This one, the voice had said. She had given an address readily, without having to think.

“What's the number here?” Ruth asked.

“Eight.”

She moved the phone away from the kettle's spout. For now she could remember both numbers. Eva set a cup beside her, and Ruth ran her thumb over the handle, finding where the glaze had chipped. Downstairs the tram pulled away. She waited for the next sound in the kitchen.

Efflorescence

The salt had grown into the phone's charging port in cubes so small and exact that Ines Calloway had to lift them out one at a time with a wooden stir stick, like picking a lock made of sugar. Under the crust the contacts had gone the green of old pennies.

She worked at Bench Three in the annex of the Harbor Street library, the last building in Marrow Point still standing above the second survey stake. On Friday the county's truck would come for the server and the boxes. Until then there were phones, dictaphones, a talking doll whose owner swore it held a message from her mother: two hundred and twelve residents' worth of things meant to be said and not. The county called it the Unsent Project, in a typeface chosen to be comforting. Ines cleaned each device with isopropyl and a toothbrush, coaxed the memory awake, copied the drafts, and tagged them: name, date, addressee if given. She trimmed the head of every file. Everyone's first four seconds were breathing.

The dehumidifier by the door shuddered and clicked; its tank filled every four hours now. White lace had crept from the laptop's fan grille onto the keys, so that typing sounded like walking on gravel. Her own phone lay face down at the far end of the bench, where it lay every Sunday until four o'clock, when her brother would call and she would let it ring, and listen to what he'd left on Monday.

The last phone in the bin wore a luggage tag in her handwriting. Calloway, I. She had put it there in February, to be fair, and hadn't looked at it since, the way you don't look at a box in your own attic. It held no photographs, no contacts, and one voice memo, six minutes and twelve seconds long, created on the eleventh of March. The calendar taped above the bench also said the eleventh of March. The year on the file was ten ahead.

Ines knew about clocks. Salt ate the little real-time batteries first; the county scanner had insisted for a month that it was 1970. She said as much to the room, and pulled the file.

The transfer died at forty-one seconds. She played what she had through the bench speaker, and her own voice came out, lower, sanded, the voice she would have had if she'd smoked, which she hadn't.

"Hi. It's me. Don't check the date; you've already checked the date. The annex is still standing. Mostly. Also, buy a second toothbrush. Not for teeth."

Ines looked at the green toothbrush in the cup by the sink. She had brushed with it that morning. She had told no one.

"You did right, staying," said the voice. "I want you to hear that from someone who—"

Then the ragged end of the file, and a hiss.

She played it four times. She did not pull the file again. She emptied the tank, and wiped the bench, and the loupe, and the bench, and practiced being a person who had been told. Staying had been the whole argument with Cal in April, the day he took the buyout. He had stood in the annex doorway with the truck idling and said she didn't have to be the memory of the place, and she had said she had forty boxes. He'd looked at her for a while. Since then, Sundays.

At three she put the phone under the loupe. The trouble was a single white cube bridging two pins. It came away on the stick like a tooth.

The second pull ran the full six minutes twelve. Her voice came back with more in it.

"—someone who knows. I say it a lot now. Out loud. The stairwell has it memorized." Behind the words, a small steady sound: water moving against something that was not going to move. "Second step. It's at my shoes. Don't fuss, it's just cold. I've got all of them, you know. Every one, dry, in order. Nobody's asked for one in years."

The voice waited. Ines could hear it deciding.

"Cal stopped calling in the fourth year. That's not a complaint. I let it ring, and then I stopped noticing it ring, and then it stopped. So." A breath, the kind she trimmed from everyone else. "Say it to someone who can say something back. Whatever it is. This isn't an emergency. I just wanted to hear how I sound when somebody's listening."

That was the message. The rest of the file was water. Four minutes of it, and once, far off, a gull, and near the very end, so quietly that she turned the gain up until the speaker crackled, her own voice saying you did right to no one, in the tone of a woman checking a lock she has already checked.

Nobody had trimmed it. Nobody had been there to.

She sat with her palms flat on the bench. A fine white bloom had started along the edge of the laptop's palm rest, patient as frost, and she watched it the way she might have watched a person work up to speaking.

At four her phone shivered against the wood. The speaker grille had furred over with salt, so the ring came thin and far off, as if from the end of a pipe. CAL. She let it go once, twice, three times, long enough to know exactly how a person learns not to hear it, and picked up on the fourth.

For the first four seconds there was only breathing, his and hers. She left it in.

Reason Not Sent

The salt had come up through the charging port overnight and spread across the board in a white fern, the way it did when a phone had wintered in a drawer in a house that had wintered in the sea. Ines Calder held it under the lamp and went at the fern with a toothbrush dipped in isopropyl. The crystals let go in a fine dust that settled on the bench, on the jam jar, on the back of her hand, so that by lunch she would be lightly cured.

The lifeboat house had the only working dehumidifier left in Lowmarsh, which was why the council had put the archive there and why the slipway doors were sandbagged from the inside. She kept the laptop in a dry box with silica packets, and the drives in a second dry box inside the first, and still every fortnight she found a bloom on a contact somewhere, small and glittering and patient.

The deposit form had a field for the speaker's name, a field for the intended recipient, and a field for Reason Not Sent. Most people left the last one blank. Mrs Kendle, bringing in two phones in a freezer bag, had filled it in for both. Recipient deceased, she had written for the first. Recipient alive, for the second.

That was the project. Lowmarsh was being given back one street at a time, red surveyor's crosses on the doors from the front to the church, the Co-op shelves bare except for the tide tables nobody had thought to take, and what the leaving residents brought her, mostly, was not photographs. It was phones. Dictaphones. A baby monitor. Devices on which, at some hour, they had pressed record and spoken to someone and not pressed send. She dried them, brushed them, coaxed the files off, logged them, and in March, when the contract ended, she would hand the drives to the county and go.

It came in without a form. A silver voice recorder no bigger than a lighter, in a sandwich bag, at the bottom of the deposit box under a car stereo. The battery hatch had corroded shut; she opened it with a dental pick and found the cell furred green and the clock contact crusted white, a little reef. Dead clocks did strange things. She had logged a phone that believed it was 1970 and a dashcam that believed in a Tuesday nine years off. So when the recorder's single file came up stamped ten years ahead of her laptop's date she noted it, date unreliable, and put on the headphones.

"Right," said her own voice. "It's me."

She took the headphones off. She looked at the slipway doors, the sandbags, the dehumidifier ticking in the corner. She put them back on.

"I'm not sending this. I know that already, so." A breath, and under it a hiss, the sound salt makes inside a microphone. "The water's at the step. I've got the key. I kept the key, I'm not giving it back. You're going to want to know if it was worth it, and I—" The hiss rose and swallowed a phrase. "—tell Dad it was the same for me. That it was the same. Right. That's all."

Forty-one seconds. She played it four times. It was her voice: the throat-clear before the right, the way so fell off the end of a sentence and lay there. It was her voice with ten years on it, and it had kept the key. It said the water was at the step, and she knew exactly which step.

Her father's house was the last on Ferry Lane, past the point where the tarmac went soft and then went under. On the second pass the you stood in the kitchen, buses idling behind him. Ellis was not being asked to come down. The later voice had left the file in the queue with the birthday songs and the cat, ten years on and still unsent.

Sera rewound to the hands, the drawer, the laugh. The water belonged to a higher street outside a room where the work was continuing. She was being asked to file.

She copied the three files into PUBLIC and left them without a covering note. Her voice could live next to the cat, the birthday, the deckhand's late sorry. Whoever came up for the crate would hear what the town had almost said, and would hear, too, that she had stayed. The laugh went back in the drawer. She shut it.

A fresh crust was starting along the case seam, a thin white ridge in the lamp. She drew the next handset over and cleared its port before the crystals could reach the date.

What the Sea Keeps

Lena pried the case off the phone with a library card, and salt fell onto the folding table like sugar. The charging port was furred white. She cleaned it with a toothpick, blew through it, and fitted the cable until the battery shuddered awake.

Around her, the Port Ansel library smelled of damp carpet and hot plastic. The shelves had been emptied in June, and now the room held only her two tables, a generator, and forty-three donated phones in numbered paper bags. Outside the taped windows, Marrow Street ran downhill toward a failing harbor. The town had voted in spring to take the buyout and retreat. The sea would have the lower streets by winter.

Her job, funded for eleven more weeks, was to save what people had meant to say: voicemails never sent, memos recorded at kitchen tables, videos that failed to upload. She cleaned each device, coaxed out an hour of power, and copied the audio to the drive. The council called it a managed aquatic transition heritage initiative, which made her want to invoice them for headaches.

She had learned the weight of hesitation. A fisherman named Eno had recorded seventeen apologies to his son, each stopping at the same sentence. Old Mrs. Vane had sung half a hymn into a flip phone and then said, "Well. That will do."

Salt kept finding the electronics. It bloomed across her laptop vents in pale ferns, silenced one headphone, filmed the inside of every case she opened. She wiped it down each morning. Each evening it had returned.

On Thursday she opened bag twenty-nine, a phone with a gull sticker half scraped from its back. Donor unknown, found in the laundromat. It woke on the third try. Its folder held one unsent memo, timestamp corrupted to zeroes, forty-one seconds long.

She pressed play, and her own voice filled her headphones.

It was older — thinner at the edges, slower, with a rasp she did not yet have. But the cadence was hers, the catch before a hard sentence. Behind it she heard gulls and water lapping wood, close and unhurried.

"Lena, it's Lena. When they tell you to haul the boxes up to the hill school — don't. I know you want to get everything. Let the sea have the small house."

She played it twice more. She told herself there were explanations: a mislabeled donor, a relative with a similar voice, a prank by the moving crews. She had no relatives here but her mother, and her mother was three years gone. She ran the archive's voice comparison tool with damp hands and watched the match score climb to ninety-eight percent and hold.

For the rest of the afternoon she worked without hearing what she saved. Don't haul the boxes. Thirteen boxes from the library's local-history room stood by the door — ledgers, photographs stuck at the corners, a ship's bell gone green — waiting for the truck to the hill school. She had promised to ride up and inventory everything twice. To hear her own future voice tell her not to bother felt like being told, from the far end of exhaustion, to lie down early. Give up, it seemed to say. Nothing you carry uphill will matter.

That evening she walked down to the small house on Marrow Street because she needed to look at something that was not a screen. Her mother's house: blue trim, salt-eaten steps, a watermark halfway up the door. Inside, the air was close and mineral. Her mother's armchair faced the window, its fabric stiff. On the kitchen counter stood four boxes Lena had packed and labeled — DISHES, PAPERS, COATS, MISC — moved from room to room for a week, as if rearrangement were rescue. She sat in the doorway and picked at the peeling paint until her thumbnail ached. Through the floorboards she felt water pushing under the street.

In the morning she avoided file twenty-nine. She digitized a boy describing his dog to his grandmother, a woman rehearsing leaving her keys in the mailbox. She wiped salt from the archive drive's seams and watched the crystals re-form almost as she watched, too fine to defeat.

At noon she put the headphones on and transcribed the message, as if transcription would tame it.

This time she heard what haste had missed. The gulls behind the voice were not distressed. The water moved slow, the way it did in the upper harbor on windless mornings. And her older voice — yesterday she had mistaken its evenness for emptiness. Now she heard something else beneath the words. Not bitterness. Relief, worn smooth. The woman speaking sounded like someone who had already decided, and was glad.

She thought of Mrs. Vane. That will do. The archive would never hold everything. It held enough: the shape of a voice trying. The ledgers by the door were mildewing at the spines. The photographs would not survive another winter wherever she put them; she had scanned the ones that would separate. She had been about to spend her last weeks hauling paper uphill to molder in a different room while the phones waited in their bags.

Don't. I know you want to get everything.

It was not surrender. It was permission.

She worked until the light went flat, copying steadily, and did not notice when the ache behind her eyes eased. At six she carried the archive drive and the laptop down to the small house, the drive wrapped in her jacket against the spray coming through the gratings. Salt had filmed the laptop's corners again; she left it there. In the kitchen she set the drive beside the boxes and played the message aloud, letting it fill the small damp room.

"Lena, it's Lena. Listen."

"I'm listening," she said, to no one, to herself, and let the rest play out over the sound of water under the floor.

When it finished, she took the marker and wrote on the top box in large letters: LEAVE. Then she untaped PAPERS, lifted out her mother's recipe tin and the photograph of the two of them on the harbor wall — the only things still dry — and put them in her bag with the drive. The rest she left stacked neatly above the watermark, where the sea would find them first.

Outside, the gulls were settling onto the drowned fence posts one by one, unhurried. She stood on the steps a while and watched them come.

# The Back Door

Ada dug the salt out of the headphone socket with a porcupine quill. The quill had come in with a woman's sewing things, wrapped in a paper marked GOOD FOR SOMETHING. When the point came away clean, Ada put the headphones on and heard a man telling his brother how cold the pork fat had to be. Cold enough to hurt your fingers, he said. On his three previous attempts at the message he'd given a temperature.

She kept all four. The little blue cassettes used to go out to the fishing boats with the mail. At the foot of each bunk there had been a machine to play them on. The tapes that people had talked into and thought better of sending had stayed in kitchen drawers for thirty years. Now the drawers were mostly out on the pavement, swollen neatly shut. People brought what they could to Ada at the branch library. She ran the tapes through a deck into the town archive, one last service for which a library card was good.

In the morning the sluice crew would pin the gates open and drive inland with everybody else. At high tide a boat would be able to tie up at the book return.

Her sister Kit had brought in the tapes from their mother's house. She'd also brought a plum and a hard-boiled egg and had peeled the egg while Ada's hands were occupied. Kit would take the north road with her husband's people. Ada's apartment assignment was south, in a building so new that on the map it was still a field.

There was half an inch of clear leader on the last cassette, then a woman's voice said, “April the sixth, twenty fifty-three.” Ada had been writing 6 APR 43 on transfer slips since breakfast. The woman had it right to within ten years.

“Listen to me giving you the date. Kit, could you send me the back door again? I've got the two knocks. I keep losing the bit that comes after.”

Ada took off the headphones. She'd been sitting crooked and her left foot had gone numb. She put it flat on the floor and listened again. There was a burr on the K of Kit, where the roof of her mouth had been split by a swing hook when she was nine. She could feel her tongue doing the work even with her mouth shut. Her voice had worn thin in places. Otherwise time seemed to have done very little to it.

She found Kit in the returns bay rolling their mother's good rug around a broomstick. “Have we still got the back door?”

“Till four. What do you want it for?”

Ada held up the portable recorder. Kit gave the rug another turn with her knee, then left it lying there.

They walked down the middle of Fore Street. On either side, the orange salvage crawler had taken the houses apart for their timber. Strips of weed rose and fell in the open foundations with the wash from the harbour. At their mother's, the front room was open to the street. Their little iron bedstead still stood upstairs against a strip of flowered paper, accessible now only by ladder. There was a ticket tied to it with string.

Ada went round by the alley and set the recorder on the step. The bottom hinge of the kitchen door had come away from the jamb. She lifted the door a little by its handle to clear the sill. With her free hand she gave the panel two knocks, like their mother's heels coming off on the step. Then she swung it shut.

The knocks were good. After them came the flat slap of a little piece of iron on wood. She tried lifting the door higher. She could hear Kit above her, climbing the ladder, the soft bump of her getting off at the top.

The third time Ada knocked, a sound came from upstairs that made her scalp draw tight. Two hollow notes, close together, then a long, almost tender complaint that died away on a squeak. She was six years old and could unclench her fists now and let her knees come apart under the sheet. Their mother had come home on her break to look in on them. In a minute she'd go back to the laundry and there'd be another whole sleep before morning.

Ada left the recorder on the step and went up. Kit was sitting on the bare bedsprings. She put her lips to the rail at the head of the bed, knocked on the tube with the pad of her thumb and drew a breath through her pinched lips. From two feet away you could hear the spit in it.

“How often?” Ada said.

“Depends. Sometimes you went straight off.”

Ada sat down beside Kit. Through the seat of her trousers she could feel the shiver in the iron as her sister did the door again.

“You must've lain there waiting to hear if I'd gone off.”

Kit rubbed the wet place on the rail with her sleeve. “I was awake anyway.”

From the street a man called up to ask if they wanted the bed. Kit looked at Ada. Ada shook her head, and the man took his ladder to the next house after they'd come down.

On the step Ada found her thumbprint picked out in square salt crystals on the recorder's glass. She rubbed it clear and played back what she'd got. There were the knocks and the hinge and the man calling up to ask if they wanted the bed. In the pause before he called a pigeon could be heard picking grit out of the gutter. She let the file save under its number. Kit had got as far as the corner.

Ada caught her up and slipped two fingers under the edge of her sleeve, where the wrist was warm. “If I ring you some night,” she said, “will you pick up?”

“Let it ring a while,” Kit said. “I can't do that hinge with my teeth out.”

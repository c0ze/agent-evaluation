# Salt Bloom

Gwen worked the white crust off the gold contact pins with a bamboo pick, careful not to score the solder underneath. Isopropyl stung a split knuckle on her left thumb. Every circuit board pulled from Carrow Reach this week had grown the same feathery rime along its seams, as if the Atlantic were already building its own reefs inside the plastic.

Beside her, the optical burner hummed on a worn bearing, pitting audio into a green-tinted quartz wafer, white needles already frosting its cooling fins. Behind the cinderblock wall, the last two seawall pumps thudded in uneven pairs, holding back the six o'clock tide.

She seated a cleaned kitchen mesh tile from Marsh Lane into the cradle and put on her cracked headphones. The municipal mesh had cached voice drafts during storm outages; half the peninsula had used it as a confessional instead, speaking into the dark and never pressing send.

*Look, Carl,* a man's voice said through a thin bed of hiss. *I know I missed the—* A breath, long and wet. *Forget it.*

Gwen tagged it `MARSH-08 / UNSENT`, queued it for the third wafer, and dropped the tile into the scrap bin. Two more followed: a woman reciting a plum-jam recipe to an estranged sister; a boy rehearsing an apology three times before quitting mid-word. Gwen kept her face still, her clipboard squared to the desk.

She had kept the same posture an hour earlier on the pier. Locking up 14 Cedar Street that morning, she had seen a pale rectangle on the kitchen plaster where their own mesh tile had been pried off, and figured the county salvage crew had beaten her to it. At the slip, with the four o'clock ferry idling against the pilings, she had handed her fifteen-year-old daughter the storage key, read the forwarding address twice, and walked back toward the annex before the gangplank lifted. Nora had stood at the rail with both hands buried in her parka pockets, jaw locked, her thumbnail tapping twice against something inside the lining—that small, nervous double-click from childhood.

One tray remained. At its bottom lay the Harbor Relay's master sump board, pulled from the pump-house cellar before brine reached the fuse box. Thick white salt crystals bridged its copper busbar like winter mold. Gwen scraped the leads clean and slid the board into the dock.

The monitor stuttered, then resolved one orphaned packet in the `14-CEDAR` slot.

`RECORDED: 2049-11-19 | STATUS: UNSENT | VOICE-MATCH: NOVAK, G. (94.2%)`

Gwen leaned back, her boot scraping wet concrete. Salt bridging a quartz oscillator could kick a clock anywhere, though an exact ten-year leap was ambitious—easily the single most punctual municipal asset in Carrow Reach.

She put the headphones on and pressed play.

No pumps hummed behind the audio—only the dry tick of a radiator and distant highway traffic.

"November nineteenth," the voice said.

Gwen's hand froze above the keyboard. It was her own voice—heavier, worn thin at the edges, carrying the dry catch before the *s* that crept into her throat past midnight.

"Ten years since the pumps quit," the voice went on. "I pulled the gray tile out of the closet tonight—the one from the kitchen wall. Even eighty miles inland, white salt still crusts the vent slots whenever it rains. Funny what refuses to leave."

A chair creaked.

"No relay left to catch this. Just dead solder and cold wire. Still, I needed to say it all the way through. I used to hate how clean you made that last afternoon—walking off the pier without looking back once, as if a town could be packed into quartz wafers and signed off on a clipboard. For years I called it coldness. Told myself you'd made peace with the water taking the street, the porch, every half-finished conversation left in the drywall."

Gwen's throat tightened. Beyond the salt-filmed glass, the five o'clock contractor barge blew a low warning across the harbor. She stared at `2049`, pressing her thumb into the split knuckle.

"Then last week," the voice dropped, "I finally pulled the Carrow Reach archive copy from storage and played the last track on the third wafer—the one with no street address. I didn't know. You weren't cold. You were just keeping your hands busy so you wouldn't break apart before the boat pulled out. I'm sorry it took ten years to forgive you for leaving so neatly. I finally understand."

Two seconds of quiet room tone, then a soft click.

Gwen dropped the headphones around her neck, hands trembling. Ten years inland, still talking to dead plastic in a quiet room, trying to absolve the cold woman who had walked away from her daughter at the rail.

She reached for the main breaker. Then stopped.

If she had carried their kitchen tile inland, it had to be in the Cedar Street bin. She dragged the damp crate closer, sifted through the tagged housings, and flipped the soaked manifest on her clipboard.

Beside `14 CEDAR — KITCHEN`, blue grease pencil read: *UNIT ABSENT*.

Gwen didn't move. The pale rectangle on the kitchen plaster. Nora at the rail, one parka pocket squared off around something small and hard.

With her sleeve, she wiped a fresh bloom of salt off the amplifier's gain dial, slid the headphones back on, scrubbed to the last three seconds, and turned the room tone up.

Right before the stop button clicked, it was there beneath the radiator's tick—two quick taps of a bitten thumbnail against hard plastic.

Slowly, Gwen looked up at the monitor.

`VOICE-MATCH: NOVAK, G. (94.2%)`

Not ten years of wear on her own throat. Ten years of a daughter growing into her mother's bones.

The barge horn blew again, shorter. On the burner, the third green wafer still spun, a narrow ring of blank glass left at its rim. Gwen opened a new track, left the street-address field empty, and pulled the battered desk microphone closer.

Her thumb pressed the red switch. White salt was already frosting the burner's vents again as she leaned toward the grille, let her shoulders drop, and began to speak.

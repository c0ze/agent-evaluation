Cover story · 01

The River Keeps Its Receipts

Words by Imani Vale / Photography by Rune Okafor / 6,100 words

Along a drought-exposed riverbed, shop ledgers, bottle caps, and vanished street plans surface together. A field essay about civic memory and what infrastructure tries to forget.

Vale spent four months walking the forty-one kilometres of bed that the water abandoned between the Kōzu bridge and the decommissioned filtration plant, recording what the mud gave back: a greengrocer’s ledger for a street that no longer exists, eleven hundred bottle caps dated before the bypass, a bus timetable laminated into the silt.

Read the opening two paragraphs

The river is a bad archivist and an honest one. It does not file; it settles. Everything the city has dropped since the Meiji culvert was poured lies in the same stratum, sorted only by weight, so a hairpin from 1953 shares a shelf with a parking stub from last Tuesday. I came here to look at drought and found instead a ledger.

At kilometre nine the bed opens into what residents call the long room — a flat expanse of cracked clay where the water used to run chest-deep. A woman named Adaku sells oranges at the rim and has watched the level fall for thirty years. “It is not that it is leaving,” she told me. “It is that it is showing us the floor.”

Pull quote

“It is not that it is leaving. It is that it is showing us the floor.”

Adaku N., greengrocer, kilometre nine

Field note

41 km walked, April–August 2026. Elevation readings taken at fourteen transects; every object catalogued in a notebook that Vale then photographed and left in the mud.

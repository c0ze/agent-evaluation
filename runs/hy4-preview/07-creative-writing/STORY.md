# Managed Retreat

The salt came first, as it always did: a grey bloom on the chrome collar of the quarter-inch jack, fine as frost on a fence wire. Nadia worked it loose with her thumbnail before she let herself touch anything else. She kept a toothbrush in the pencil drawer for the rest of it — the card slots, the pins, the little gold teeth of the hub. Salt got into everything. Salt had opinions about the future.

The archive occupied the second floor of the Killick library, which had been a house once and would be a shoal eventually; up here the sills were still above the spray. Downstairs, what was left of the town's furniture stood in labeled rows waiting for trucks. The state called this a managed retreat, which was the phrase it used for anything it could not stop. Nadia's job was narrower. On Tuesdays and Thursdays the people of Killick came up the stair and sat in the chair beside the microphone and recorded messages they did not intend to send. Eleven hundred hours of it so far. The grant called the work intangible heritage preservation. Nadia called it Mr. Pell's fourteenth attempt to explain a Tupperware lid to his brother.

Then the queue coughed up a file it should not have had.

Metadata first, the only honest part of the job. Duration 4:11. Date recorded: a Tuesday in March, ten years ahead. Her initials in the operator field, typed the way she typed them.

She played it.

"It's me," her own voice said, and it was hers and it was not — hers the way a photograph is, taken from a distance she had never agreed to: thinner at the edges, with a small flat hum behind it she could not place.

"The Ardens' house goes on the second March tide. Not the first. You'll want to be there. Don't go down to the water." A pause, tape-ish, although there was no tape. "Don't rescue the Rickenbacker. You'll want to. Let it go. Say yes to the woman with the ferry." Then: "Record forty minutes of nothing. I'd give anything for forty minutes of nothing." And then the smallest thing, nearly lost: "It was worth it. Even the end part."

Nadia sat with her hands flat on the desk until the dehumidifier clicked off.

She did what anyone does: she made a list. At first the message was a manual written backwards, instructions from somebody who had already failed: don't go, let go, say yes. She underlined *Don't go down to the water* twice. She noticed the contradiction — you'll want to be there, don't go — and let it stand.

Everyone assumed the February storm would finish the house on Pike, since February had taken its steps and half its dune. February did not finish it. The second tide in March did, at four in the afternoon, in eleven minutes, with a sound like a zipper.

After that, Nadia believed, the way you believe a diagnosis. She stopped walking the shore after work. She let the Rickenbacker — the Ardens' boy's guitar, in its case on the porch, which was to say under the porch — she let it stay. And when Tess, who ran the ferry out to the survey markers and had been asking Nadia out on the water for the better part of a year, asked again at the mailboxes, Nadia heard herself say, "I've got work," and heard, underneath it, the real reason: she would not be moved toward a woman by a voice in a file.

Tess said *sure* in the particular way she said it. Nadia went upstairs and sat with her hands flat on the desk again.

The file degraded. Everything did; the salt came up through the floor and went looking for current. By April the flat hum behind her voice had thickened to a hiss and the line she most needed had gone soft at both ends, so that *Don't go down to the water* arrived as *Don't go — to the water*, with one word the salt had eaten.

She took the deck apart on a Thursday: boards, ribbon cable, and across the head a film of white like a breath held too long. She brushed it the way you would brush a child's hair, and set the file to play.

"The Ardens' house goes on the second March tide. Not the first. You'll want to be there. Don't go down to the water without the recorder."

Oh, she thought. Oh.

She played it twice, and the whole thing turned over in her hands like a stone with a second side.

Not a warning. A commission. The future had not sent back a way to survive; it had sent back a list of what it could no longer have, in the only order a person in a hurry would say it: the date, the sound, the one thing worth carrying. Don't go down without the recorder. Let the guitar go — you'll want to; it's all right. Say yes. This was not a woman escaped from a drowning town calling back to a woman about to drown in it. It was a woman on some inland street with no water in it, ten years gone, who wanted forty minutes of a room with west windows and a dehumidifier clicking every eleven minutes, and the only person alive who could get it for her was the one who had never left.

"It was worth it," her older voice said, nearly lost. "Even the end part."

Nadia put the deck back together, checked the level on the handheld, stretched a fresh foam windscreen over the microphone, and took the stairs two at a time, past the labeled furniture, out into the long flat light. At the landing Tess was idling, engine knocking over. Nadia held the recorder up so Tess could see it, and Tess, whose *sure* had worn thin as a coin, said it once more, and this time Nadia said, "Yes. But I'm working."

The house on Pike was gone; the water came in and did its small arithmetic on the stones. Nadia sat on the thwart with the handheld balanced on her knee and recorded the engine, the gulls, the slap under the hull, Tess saying something she could not make out and did not want to. She watched the needle and gave the future forty minutes of nothing at all, and on the grey foam of the windscreen, in the air coming off the water, the salt began again.

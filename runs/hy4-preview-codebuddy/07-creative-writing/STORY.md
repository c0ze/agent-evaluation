# Managed Retreat

Mara cleaned the contacts with a toothbrush dipped in isopropyl, exactly as the
manual prescribed, though the manual had been written for machines that lived
inland. The salt came back anyway: a pale fur along the solder joints, a gritty
bloom across the heat sink, the faint sugar-crackle she could hear when she
seated a ribbon cable. The sea was inside the exchange now, and it had, by all
evidence, nothing but time.

The exchange was a concrete room under the public library. It held nine racks of
a thing the county still called, in its paperwork, the Line. For forty-one years
a person in Sable Harbor could dial a number and say the thing they had been
unable to say to a face — the widow to her husband, the son to his father, the
man at the marina to the Coast Guard about a light he had not reported. Nothing
was delivered. That was the service. The Line only kept things. Now the buyouts
were final, the road out was being regraded, and the county had contracted Mara
for eleven weeks to make a copy that would outlive the room.

She had finished racks one and two. On the fourth morning of rack three, the
queue surfaced a file whose caller field read HOLLIS, MARA — her own name, set
down in the same terse order she had typed two thousand times, surname first —
and whose date was ten years ahead of the day.

She checked the header twice. Then she put on the headphones.

"It's you. Don't panic, and don't tell anyone, because they'll tell you to stop,
and you won't. Keep going until the third rack is done. Then walk out, and don't
come back for the rest. I know how that sounds. It's not a kindness, but it's
the truth: you can't save all of it. You can only save what you finish. And —
when you get to Yvonne Pike's message, the one about her brother, don't —"

It ended mid-word, the way a recording ends when a thumb lifts.

She sat with the headphones on for a while, listening to nothing. Then she did
the only thing available to her, which was to treat it as an order and obey it.
She worked. Reel out, label, transfer, checksum, reel back, the dehumidifier's
tube dripping into a bucket whose rim had grown a white collar. She ate at the
bench with one hand and kept the other on the transport. When her phone stopped
charging she turned it over, saw the port packed with crystals, and cleaned it
with the same toothbrush, which seemed to her a reasonable arrangement of the
world's sorrows.

The county's retreat coordinator had taped a card to the rack beside hers, in
case anyone forgot: IN CASE OF FLOOD, UNPLUG. Someone had added a second line
beneath it in ballpoint. IN CASE OF FLOOD, LEAVE. The county had paid for the
first line.

Yvonne Pike's message came up in the middle of the afternoon, catalogued under
PIKE, YVONNE — R. Her voice was old and steady and entirely unconvincing.
"Ray. I sold the boat. I know what I said. But the yard wanted storage and the
storage wanted money and — " a breath, a rearrangement of the mouth, " — and it
was only a boat. Everything here is fine. Everything here is fine." She hung up
without saying goodbye, because with the Line you never had to.

Mara logged it, checksummed it, and let it go into the copy. She did not add a
note. She did not mark it as untrue, though it plainly was, and though she had
marked others. Then she sat back and understood, without any particular
ceremony, that the future had sent her a woman lying to her drowned brother on
purpose, and had told her to keep going, and had not told her why.

The tape was in her hand. That was the part she had walked past twice. The file
had not arrived over a network. It had come out of rack three, off a reel with a
volunteer's cursive label, from a shelf she had not yet emptied — a shelf she
would have reached on the fourth morning of the third rack, at the hour when the
work always turned to grit and she always thought about the drive out.

Someone had put it there. Someone had known which shelf.

"You can only save what you finish," she said aloud, to the room, and heard it
differently the second time. Not triage. Not a captain's arithmetic over a
flooded town. A woman ten years on, saying: we did not save the rest. This is
what is left of us, and it is only what you finish, and I need you to finish.

And *don't come back for the rest* — she had heard it as a threat, or as a
prediction of water. It was neither. It was permission. Mara had watched the
holdouts on Halloway Point nail plywood over windows they had already sold.
She had known, without deciding anything, that she was capable of the same
stubbornness, of staying with a thing past the point where staying was sense.
The voice on the tape was not ordering her off the sinking ground. It was
releasing her from it, from someone who knew how hard the leaving would be.

She finished rack three at ten past seven. The last reel was a man asking his
mother whether she had been afraid, and then answering for her, kindly, no.

In the booth by the door she keyed the Line and recorded a reply. She kept it
short, because there was no knowing how much anyone could carry. She said that
she had finished the third rack. She said that she was walking out now. She said
that she was not going to say what to do with the rest.

She set the toothbrush in the jar by the door, bristles up, the way you leave a
thing for the next person. The latch of the exchange door had furred white again
since morning. Mara watched her own hand on it, salt on salt, and did not wipe
it, and went up the stairs.

# The Unsent Room

The salt showed up first along the seams of the telephone exchange’s steel desk, a fine white fringe that returned no matter how often Mara wiped it away. By noon it had gathered in the charging port of her field recorder. She teased it out with the corner of a paper label, careful not to bend the little copper tongue.

Outside, the town had been reduced to decisions. Blue paint marked the buildings cleared for this winter’s move inland; orange paint marked those no one would pay to lift. At the exchange, a line of residents had left old handsets, answering-machine tapes, and phone backups in grocery bags. Their last request to the municipal archive was the same: digitize the unsent messages, but don’t deliver them.

Mara was good at not asking why. She named each file, checked the waveform, and entered the address only when the speaker had provided one. Most people left that box empty.

The recordings were small rooms she could not enter: a man reminding his brother to oil a hinge; a teenager practicing an apology, stopping every time she reached the name. Mrs. Vale, who had run the lunch counter by the ferry, said, “If Len asks, I never liked his new kitchen. It’s so small we’ll have to call it a pantry and charge it rent.” Mara smiled despite herself, then saved the clip under UNCLAIMED. It was the day’s only joke with a clean ending.

She kept the exchange lights on after the public power schedule dropped to evening-only. A battery pack hummed beneath the desk. When the metal shutters rattled, salt sifted from their tracks and stippled the black keyboard. She brushed it away with the flat of her hand, leaving damp crescents.

The file appeared at 16:12, between an unanswered message from a child and six seconds of somebody breathing before hanging up. Its timestamp read 2046-09-30. The clock on the wall said 2036-09-30. The file had no sender field, no address, no device tag. Just a waveform and a name: MARA.

She put on the headphones.

“Don’t wait for me,” her own voice said. Older by some unmeasurable fraction, or perhaps only tired. A slight catch before the second sentence, one she knew from listening to herself dictate catalog notes. “Keep me here. I’m coming home.”

Then the recording ended.

Mara listened again. A dry click followed the last word, as if a switch had been turned off. She examined the file header. The date was intact; the checksum was not. Salt had clouded one contact on the transfer hub, and it could have scrambled the metadata. She wrote that down, though the voice had passed through untouched.

For three nights she dreamed of the exchange with all its doors locked and its lights burning under water. In the morning she carried the file to the high school gym, where the relocation office had set up cots and cardboard boxes. She did not play it for anyone. The future had offered no explanation, only an instruction she could obey in the wrong way.

By Friday the archive’s deadline had become a stripe of red tape across the front door: EQUIPMENT REMOVAL, MONDAY. Mara had set aside one transport case for the drives and another for the old answering-machine tapes that had not survived transfer. The town itself would be left to the tide line and the caretakers’ survey stakes.

A woman from the last block came in carrying a cracked telephone in both hands. She left a message for a son who had moved inland when he was twelve. She didn’t give an address.

“He knows where I am,” she told Mara, then looked at the screen as though the words had appeared there. “I mean, he did.”

Mara nodded. She had learned not to repair the sentence after people let it go.

That night, she ran the full archive index. Voices appeared in columns: dates, lengths, names, no destination. The unsent ones did not lack a way out. They had been left unsent on purpose. Still, she had thought of the empty address field as a kind of waiting, a door someone might come back to open.

Her own message was at the bottom of the queue, listed under the archive’s collection rather than her name. She understood then what she had mistaken. “Keep me here” had sounded like a request to save a room, to stay beside the exchange while the sea took the lower streets. But the voice was already here, among the voices she had been paid to keep. “Coming home” did not require the building to survive. It did not even require an answer.

Mara changed the entry from TEMPORARY to UNSENT, left the address blank, and added the file to the export. She packed the drives beside the old tapes, wrapping each in the dry cotton shirts she had brought for herself. The future recording went between Mrs. Vale’s joke and the child’s unfinished breath.

At dawn, she shut off the exchange lights. The black keyboard was freckled again with salt, crystals bright in the dust. Mara wiped a clean space for the door key, set it there for the caretaker, and lifted the case.

She left the room with them.

The arc runs from holding on to being allowed to stop: Ilse has buried herself in indexing the dead because the work is the only thing left with subscribers on it, and the future message is first heard as proof that the work will have been worth it and that she survives inside it. Hearing the rest of the recording — "let the water have the rest of it" — turns it from prophecy into permission, so saving four hundred and ten people becomes a night of carrying rather than a lifetime of counting. The dry humor and the salt keep the grief physical and unsentimental rather than elegiac.

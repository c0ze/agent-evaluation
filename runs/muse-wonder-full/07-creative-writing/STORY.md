# What the Salt Keeps

Mara brushed salt out of the card reader with a child's toothbrush before trusting it with anybody's voice. The crystals returned nightly, fine as ground glass along the contacts. She worked over a tea towel so the grit would show, then slid in the first card.

The archive occupied the old lifeboat station, whose concrete floor already knew how to be wet. Milk crates along the ramp held smashed handsets, dictaphones taped shut, phones labelled in marker at the relocation office. Everything smelled of vinegar and warm plastic.

Keelwick had been given a date two winters ago, the way a patient is given one. After September the bus would stop at the ridge, gangs would lift the lower causeway, and the sea would be allowed the streets it borrowed at high tide. Many residents had recorded messages they never sent. The council called this memory capture. Mara logged it as *unsent*.

Mrs. Adeyemi, seventy-nine, told a sister dead since 2029 that the fig tree had finally fruited. A trawlerman rehearsed an apology to his son, lost his place, began again. A girl whispered how to feed a cat whose name also appeared on a vet's cremation tag in the same crate. Mara listened once, logged duration and condition, and transcribed only if a family asked.

The yellow handset came in crate 31, between a baby monitor and a phone with sand rattling in its speaker. No name, no SIM. Salt had forced its cracked case apart at one corner. She picked the charging port clean with a dental probe. The screen showed one file, forty-one seconds long: *MARA_FINAL_final*. Created 12 May 2046.

Her phone insisted it was 2036. A prank, perhaps, with a wrong date. Then she pressed play and stood with one glove on, one off. The voice cleared its throat twice, as she did before something rehearsed. "It's you. Older, obviously. Don't send it. He comes anyway, and he brings the van. Eat something that isn't from a packet. That's — that's all I can fit. Mind your thumb." Under the hiss, a spoon settled in a sink.

She played it again. The voice had her flattened vowels and her habit of dropping the ends of embarrassing sentences. There was a catch before *van*, as if the speaker had almost laughed. Mara heard it as a warning's rough edge.

*Don't send it. He comes anyway.* Tomas. It could only be Tomas.

Her own phone, sealed nightly in a sandwich bag, held a message recorded in March and re-recorded eleven times. In it she explained, in order, their mother's last winter on Harbor Row, the fall by the coal scuttle, the kettle boiled dry and still hot. She said *you chose not to come*, then *I chose not to tell you how bad it was until it was an accusation*. It was a good message. That was the trouble; it had paragraphs. She meant to include it in the final upload, released automatically on closure day, after the station phone was disconnected.

Now her older self told her not to send it, and that it would make no difference. Tomas would come anyway, with his van, presumably for their mother's dresser and pans, the things he had circled two years ago in a solicitor's PDF.

She logged the handset. The council spreadsheet offered *flood*, *mould*, *battery*, and *other*. She selected *flood*. In Keelwick that covered most metaphysics.

For three days she worked around crate 31. Salt starred the monitor and jammed her space bar; she lifted the key and blew out crystals, tasting them. A Harbor Row card failed midway, a man's voice breaking into bright shards. She wrote *partial* and hated the word. Cutting tape on crate 34, she opened her thumb on the green-handled knife. Sitting on an upturned bucket with blue paper towel pressed to it, she said, "Mind your thumb. Right. Thanks."

On Friday the council phoned: divers had found the causeway undermined. It would close Sunday night, a week early. They had rung her emergency contact about shifting the last crates to the ridge. She was still absorbing *emergency contact* when an engine reversed down the ramp.

Tomas's van had one white door and salt lace around its windscreen. He got out with two paper cups and the face he used for parking tickets. "You could have just told me the lift was broken."

"There isn't a lift. It's a lifeboat station."

"Explains the ramp." He gave her tea, real tea, not packet soup. "Council said twelve crates."

"Nine. Three are returns."

He had brought no solicitor, no straps for dressers, only work gloves in his back pocket. Already he was squinting at her labels, glasses forgotten in the cab.

They worked. He carried; she checked seals. When the dehumidifier alarmed, he emptied it unasked, hip to the counter because of his knee, as at their mother's. Twice she nearly mentioned Harbor Row. Once, carrying crate 31 between them, she nearly said she had recorded him something.

At noon the van would not start. Corrosion furred thick as coral around the battery clamp — salt again, patient as frost. Mara fetched the child's toothbrush. Tomas held the torch. "You still do that. Mam's radio, every Sunday."

"It still works." With her hands busy, she could say the rest. "I didn't tell you. About the kettle. How often. I saved it until it was evidence."

"I saved up not coming," he said. "Probably we could —" The engine caught, second turn, and neither finished a sentence over its noise.

That night she cleaned the yellow handset's contacts and listened once more before logging it unsent, no release, no transcription. *Don't send it. He comes anyway, and he brings the van.*

The catch before *van* was a laugh. Braced for ten years' regret, she had misheard it. The words had never meant *whatever you do, it ends badly*. They meant *put that polished thing down; you won't need it; he is already on his way with tea*. Her older self had not been warning her. She had been trying not to give the ending away.

Mara deleted her eleven-take draft. Then she set the handset, dark, on the tea towel, where by morning salt would seal its cracked corner white again, neat as a healed thing. She left it there to happen.

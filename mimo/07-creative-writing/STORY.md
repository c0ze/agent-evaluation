# Saltwater Static

The Marantz had grown a white rind along the pinch rollers. Lena Rourke worked at it with a fingernail, then a pencil eraser, then swabbed the play head with the cheap isopropyl from the go-bag. The good alcohol was for the archival deck. The cheap stuff was for anything the sea had already touched.

Page 11 of the buyout packet, wedged between the flood-elevation survey and the direct-deposit form, offered every resident one final municipal service: record a message to someone you are not going to tell. The county called it Legacy Voice. The booth's bolted-on sign, older than the program, still read PLEASE NO FISH CLEANING.

Lena's work was to ingest, normalize, and preserve. Four hundred and twelve voices out of Calder's Landing so far. Most of them were about mothers. The rest were about houses.

She ran the booth from the old ferry terminal — a fiberglass hunting blind on a concrete apron, smelling faintly of tackle and damp carpet. Each morning she opened the battery doors of whatever had come in overnight and picked the salt out of the contacts. Crystals grew in the seams overnight, in the screw heads, in the hairline gap between a drowned phone's screen and its bezel. Miss a spot and you could hear it in the files: a faint granular hiss under the voice, like someone chewing sugar.

Today's bin held nine phones, two microcassettes, a hearing aid — logged and boxed; not a recorder, but people left what they had — and a gray Olympus dictaphone with no owner tag.

The Olympus had three files. The first two were a man's voice reading out the names of people he'd worked with at the fish plant, slowly, as if for a spelling test. The third file had Lena's voice on it.

She knew the cadence of her own sentences the way she knew the sound of her own keys in a lock. This was her voice with more weather in it: lower, slower, roughened at the edges.

"Lena. If you're me, you've already checked the header. Don't bother — I did too. It's clean. — Okay. When she calls, let it ring until the room gives up. Then say the true thing. Not the careful one. The careful one is how you end up alone in a dry kitchen reading flood maps for fun. And don't let them price the van at scrap. Not because it's worth more. Because you'll need somewhere to sit. — That's all. The salt's in the Marantz again. It's always in the Marantz."

Timestamp: the fourteenth of March, 2036. Ten years and a week out from the afternoon she sat there.

Lena did not believe in it. She checked the header anyway. Then she ran the file through the spectral comparison tool she kept for relatives who disputed a voice. It matched her at ninety-eight percent, the two percent being time.

On the first pass she heard a memo. *She* was Helen in county acquisitions, who called on Thursdays about the van. *Let it ring* was advice about negotiation. *The true thing* was the true number on the odometer. *Dry kitchen* was Helen's kitchen, which Lena had seen once, with the blinds always down.

It was a workable reading. It lasted about forty minutes.

Then Ruth called the booth line — Ruth had the number because Ruth had Nell — and Lena let it ring, because she was mid-ingest, and the room went very quiet and gave up, and afterward she sat with her hands flat on the desk while the Marantz made its small cooling sounds.

Nell was nine. Inland with Ruth for the school year. In September Lena had said *after the archive closes*, and then *after the last batch*, and then *after the new year*; the new year had come in with the water in the basements of the third row of houses. Nell had stopped asking in February. That was worse.

The careful one. Lena had a repertoire. *They need me for the inventory. The road's bad. I'll know more on Friday.* Each of them true, and none of them the true thing.

The true thing was longer and uglier and did not fit on a voicemail: that she kept sitting in a tackle-smelling box collecting the goodbyes of strangers because it was the only work left here that wasn't leaving, and she did not know how to walk away from the last of it, and that if she sat here another week Nell would stop expecting her in the doorway and would have earned the right to.

She replayed the file. This time *she* was Nell at nine, at nineteen, at twenty-nine. *Let it ring until the room gives up* was how a girl learns to stop waiting. *Say the true thing* was permission. *The careful one is how you end up alone in a dry kitchen* was not a note about Helen's countertops. It was a description of an afternoon: flood maps, no one to read the high-water mark to.

There was nothing supernatural in the voice. It was only a woman alone in a dry place, ten years on, still salt-averse, still doing the inventory math on a van she hadn't sold.

Lena took the pencil eraser to the Olympus's battery contacts, where a crystal was pushing up under the spring. It flaked away and began, at once, to come back.

She left the ingest log open. She left the good alcohol on the bench. She dialed Ruth's number from memory.

On the fourth ring she was ready to say the careful thing.

"Hi, sweetheart," she said. "It's Mom. I'm not going to make you guess anymore. I'm coming Friday, and then we'll find a dry kitchen somewhere and I'll listen to you until you're sick of me."

Salt had gotten into the Marantz again. It was always getting into the Marantz. She would wipe it on Friday, or she wouldn't. Either way the rind would take her prints.

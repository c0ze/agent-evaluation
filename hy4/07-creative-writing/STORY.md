# Salt Bloom

The salt came back overnight. It always did. Nell took the phone from the biscuit tin, turned it until the light caught the charging port, and worked a wooden toothpick along the seam. A white crust gave way in grits onto the tray. For the bloom across the case she used a toothbrush gone soft. Three phones and a dictaphone waited in the tin, each with masking tape and a number. Nineteen was worst: the crystals crunched as the connector went in, like a biscuit breaking.

The council had issued her a dehumidifier rated for half this room, which she supposed was forward planning.

It had been the library reading room. Now the shelves held card boxes and labelled drives, and the radiator had gone for the dehumidifier's tube, dripping into a bucket. Through the tall windows she could read the numbers on the kerbs — 12, 14, 16 — put there for the crews. The houses behind them were empty. The vans had stopped in September.

The council called it the Record of Leaving. The brief said *oral materials of the retiring community*. In practice people handed her devices: old phones, an answering machine, a child's recorder shaped like a whale. Buried among the photographs were memos recorded and never sent. She catalogued them on cards — owner, device, date, duration — and in the last column wrote *unsent* two hundred and eleven times.

A man from Fennel Lane recorded an apology to his brother seven times, each shorter than the last; the seventh was four seconds and a click. A woman sang two lines to a daughter who had not come to the door in six years, then said "No," and stopped. Nell never trimmed the silence at the end. It seemed the truest part, the seconds after a person decided not to say any more.

Salt kept things and salt ate things, and she had never separated them. It drew the damp out of a board and left a white map across the contacts; it fogged a screen until a face looked out through gauze; it made a tidemark along the caddies. Every morning she cleaned what she had cleaned the day before. In the afternoons the drives ticked like something cooling.

One device had no number. It had been on the desk the first morning, before she was given the key: a small silver recorder in a padded envelope, no note, the flap tacky with damp. She wrote *22* on a strip of tape, put it at the bottom of the tin, and kept not reaching it.

When she finally connected it, the port was clean — the only clean one — and the file came across with its metadata intact. The date field read 04.11.36. Her log lay open at 04.11.26. She checked the recorder, the calendar, the phone in her pocket, though none had anything to do with that number. The waveform was nine seconds. She put the headphones on.

"Don't send it," her own voice said. Then a breath, and the catch at the back of the throat she had heard on the few recordings ever made of her. "It was never for anyone."

The voice was hers, gone slightly rough, the vowels worn narrow the way voices do when used less. Ten years of it sat between the sentences. She took off the headphones. The room went on ticking.

She played it four times. Between the third and the fourth she wiped the contacts of a phone already cleaned, though nothing was on them. She did not write a card. She copied it onto its own drive and carried it in her pocket, a hard square against her hip.

On her own phone there was a memo eleven seconds long, recorded six times, never sent or deleted. Her sister rang on Sunday evenings from the new estate above the bypass; they talked about the roof, the road, the forms. Afterwards Nell would open the memo, listen to the first second, and close it. She set the phone face down.

By the end of the week she had decided, without deciding, that it was a warning. There was a last transfer on Friday: the catalogue going up the line before the exchange closed. She had the upload queued and kept finding reasons not to plug in. It sounded like somebody ten years older who knew how it turned out and had called back to save her the trouble — who knew that no one would open the files, that two hundred and eleven people saying nothing to nobody had been a waste of a winter. She heard it in the dehumidifier's click, in the pause before the kettle boiled.

Number 14 had kept its curtains. On Thursday Mr. Coyle came in with a biscuit tin of his own and set it on the desk, keeping his hand on the lid. "She made them after I'd gone up," he said. "I could hear her from the front room. Never loud." He looked at the rack of drives. "Will they go to anyone?"

"They'll be kept," Nell said. It was the only true thing she had.

"Oh, she wasn't talking to me." He said it without weight, the way you'd correct a date. "She just liked to have said it." He buttoned his coat. Outside, the water had found the second step.

After he had gone she sat with the drives ticking. Then she took the square from her pocket and played it once more without the headphones, holding the recorder up so the sound came thin into the room. It didn't sound like a warning now. It sounded like permission.

She wrote the card. Owner, device, date, duration; last column, *unsent*. Under recipient she drew a line and left it empty. She set the recorder beside the whale. On Friday she sent the catalogue up the line. It took eleven minutes. When the bar reached the end the fan went quiet and the room was as it had been. Her own memo stayed on her phone. She did not delete it. She did not send it.

Before she locked up she wiped the seam of the recorder and emptied the bucket into the car park, where the tide had been. She walked home with an empty coat pocket. By morning there would be a fine white line along the seam again; a fine white line along everything, the caddies, the window latch, the jar. She would take the toothbrush to it, as every morning, with the window open to the water.

# The Salt Archive

Mara kept the dead phones in biscuit tins.

The town hall back room smelled of damp cardboard and hot plastic. She worked at a folding table with a cracked leg, feeding devices into the extractor one by one: handsets with swollen batteries, a warped tablet, two sat-phones from the ferry crew. The extractor hummed and sweated. Salt had bloomed overnight along its vents — fine white crystals beading like frost. She brushed them off with a toothbrush she kept for that purpose. They ticked onto the table.

Outside, the lower road had gone last spring. The houses on Mariner's Row stood with their doors open, curtains still hung, waiting for the buyout crews or the tide, whichever arrived first. Mara had stopped knocking before entering. Everyone left things behind they meant to send.

Her job, funded eleven more weeks by the relocation office, was simple: recover unsent voice messages, tag them with name and address, upload them before the servers moved inland. Most were ordinary. *Mum, the van's full, we'll call from Taunton.* A man singing happy birthday to someone named Ellis, stopping halfway to cough. A teenager dictating a grocery list, then adding, after a long pause, *never mind*.

On Tuesday she opened a tin labeled 14 HARBOUR VIEW — J. ELDRED, and found a field recorder.

Its rubber casing had gone sticky. Salt crusted the microphone mesh and the seam around the memory slot. She knew the model; she owned one just like it, down to the frayed lanyard. She turned hers over where it lay beside the keyboard. Same scratch across the back. Same tape holding the battery door shut.

She told herself the county bought in bulk.

The recorder held forty-one files. Forty were gull cries, boat horns, an old woman describing the proper way to bottle plums. The forty-first was dated ten years from now.

Mara checked twice. The extractor sometimes misread corrupted headers — it had once dated a pizza order to 1974. She pulled the file manually and read the hex. The timestamp held.

She put on headphones.

Her own voice said: "If you're hearing this, don't bring the tins up the hill."

A pause. Breath. Behind it, water lapping close, a radio murmuring numbers.

"They'll tell you it's safer. The dry room, the dehumidifiers. They mean well. But the salt gets in anyway, and then you won't have — " Static ate the next words. "Just leave them where the tide can reach. Let them stay. Please."

Mara took the headphones off. Across the table, her own recorder's red light blinked low-battery, patient as an animal. She cleaned the extractor vent again, though she'd already cleaned it. The crystals came away under her thumb with a faint crunch.

She did not believe in messages from the future. She believed in bit rot, header errors, tired women misreading screens in mildewed rooms. She copied the file to quarantine and flagged it CORRUPT/TIMESTAMP.

That night she carried the tins upstairs to the flat above the hall, all six, because the ground floor had taken two centimeters at high tide and Pritchard from the relocation office, a kind man who wore his lanyard over a life vest, had begged her to. "Dry room upstairs. Dehumidifier. You'll thank me."

"Nothing thanks anyone anymore," Mara said. "The toaster's given up even beeping at me."

She ate beans standing up, listening to the building settle. Through the window, one solar lamp someone had left in a drowned garden on Mariner's Row threw a small, stubborn circle onto the water. She thought about the voice — how hoarse it had sounded, how careful, like someone choosing words that had to pass through a narrow place.

In the morning the extractor wouldn't power on. Salt had furred over the plug prongs. She scraped it with a knife and worked through the backlog. Mrs. Anand, 6 Cliff Path: *Tell your father the roses are in the van, never mind the pots.* A boy to his teacher: *I finished the book, the ending was rubbish, thank you.* She tagged and uploaded, and tried not to replay the forty-first file.

On Thursday the tide came over the seawall at noon and sat in the high street till evening. Pritchard waded down in waders, carrying sandbags he clearly knew were theatrical.

"Let me take those tins to the dry room at the school," he said, eyeing the stack she'd brought back downstairs that morning without quite deciding to. "That flat will steam them."

"It's a digitization project, not a preservation project," Mara said. "Everything's in the cloud. The cloud is famously never wet."

He laughed, surprised, and left the sandbags anyway.

After he left she played the message again. This time she heard what the static had covered: a small metallic ticking underneath, rhythmic. Like someone brushing salt off a grille with a toothbrush. Tick. Tick. Tick.

She looked at her recorder. She had left it running that morning out of habit, capturing room tone for the archive — these old buildings sounded different, she'd told Pritchard; future people might want to know.

She stopped the recording and checked the file list. Forty-one files. Her throat went dry.

The newest was dated ten years from now.

She understood all at once, with the physical certainty of touching a live wire: she had not received a warning. She had received an instruction she was already following and would keep following, looped through salt and habit. Every few years she would digitize the tins, find the message, carry it upstairs or down, brush the crystals off the vents, leave her recorder running. The plea wasn't *don't save them*. It was *don't take them somewhere they can't decay*. Leave them where the tide can reach. Let the archive stay mortal.

She sat a long time. The extractor hummed. Salt gathered invisibly on everything, atom by atom, out of the air itself.

Then she picked up her recorder and recorded over the future file: gulls, the extractor's hum, water in the street outside. Her own breathing. At the end she added, for whoever catalogued it next: "The plums. Plenty of sugar. Don't skimp."

She saved it, stripped the date as best she could, and set the recorder in the tin with the others, downstairs, where the tide could reach.

# Outbox

Sera eased the SIM tray out with a guitar pick and tapped the salt into a chipped mug already mapped with it, gray from the docks, white from the handsets. The mug sat on the reading-room table between a crate stenciled IN and a crate stenciled PUBLIC. The drawer under the table had no stencil. She had decided a name would turn it into a rule.

The ferry-office phone belonged to the mesh the county had bolted to the pilings the year the postal truck stopped coming. It took a charge only if she pinched the cable at a tired angle. A white bloom had crept under the glass at one corner, crystals lifting the display a hair. She had logged bad dates before. Phones gave her 1970. Phones gave her next Thursday. She noted them, corrected what she could, and kept the voices.

This date sat ten years out. She subtracted it twice. The recording opened on room tone she did not have: a soft, regular slap of water against something hollow, close to the mic. Then her voice, lower, with the old click of tooth on tooth she made when she was keeping a sentence small.

"Put this with the others. Don't leave it in the drawer. I stayed because the work gave my hands somewhere to be. You were right. They could have worked anywhere. I don't want a rescue and I don't want a verdict. Open the archive. Mine too. I'm tired of being the only one who knows what people almost said. Let them be overheard. If you still need one private ending, keep the laugh. The salt hasn't taken it. I checked this morning."

She set the phone down without letting the cable slip.

The drawer held a drive the size of a cough drop. On it were three files with Ellis's name in them and a fourth she had never meant to keep: her own laugh, caught while she knocked a microphone against the radiator to see if it was alive. Two seconds. Salt under the glass had laced the waveform with bright ticks. The laugh still played, surprised at itself.

She had told Ellis she would take the inland road the morning he did. She had stayed at the window instead and become a person who saved drafts. Hearing that person, older, tell her to empty the drawer felt like a verdict wearing good manners. Open the archive meant the nights she had practiced being forgiven would sit where anyone could press play. I don't want a rescue meant the water in the recording had already finished the argument. Last week's tide had dried on the south wall in a tea-colored map below the sill. The future file put the sea in the room regardless.

"No," she said, to the year on the screen.

Work was simpler than an answer. A child's handset held a birthday song with the name wrong and the tune careful and a little flat. A deckhand had recorded that the lower steps were slick, and only afterward apologized for his language, the apology too late to cover the warning. Then a woman, level as a receipt:

"Greg. I have the cat. This is not a negotiation. The cat's vote has been counted."

Sera typed resolved and took the next phone.

Her thumbnail cracked a white crust off its port. Underneath, the metal was raw copper. On the ferry handset the speaker mesh had grown its own crust, rough under her finger, the kind that took clocks and left the harder consonants. She played the future file again. The same grit ticked. Under it the water kept its patient time.

Ellis had said the line about her hands in the kitchen, the buses idling on the ridge in a long pale row. You could do this anywhere. He had picked up his mug, come back a minute later for the charger, and gone up the road with the charger dangling from his pocket, the mug forgotten on the step. She had brought his mug in. It was the one on the table now, holding the salt. She had recorded three answers after the engines thinned out, and sent none.

You were right.

She had taken the you for herself. A woman with her tooth-click and a worse window was telling her she had been wrong to stay, and that the water would finish the correction. On the second pass the you stood in the kitchen, buses idling behind him. Ellis was not being asked to come down. The later voice had left the file in the queue with the birthday songs and the cat, ten years on and still unsent.

Sera rewound to the hands, the drawer, the laugh. The water belonged to a higher street outside a room where the work was continuing. She was being asked to file.

She copied the three files into PUBLIC and left them without a covering note. Her voice could live next to the cat, the birthday, the deckhand's late sorry. Whoever came up for the crate would hear what the town had almost said, and would hear, too, that she had stayed. The laugh went back in the drawer. She shut it.

Out on the pilings the tide kept the old slow meter. The meter inside the recording was quicker, a smaller room. She pinched the cable until the timestamp dimmed. A fresh crust was starting along the case seam, a thin white ridge in the lamp. She drew the next handset over and cleared its port before the crystals could reach the date.
